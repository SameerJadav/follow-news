China has warned that it could take retaliatory measures if the United States significantly expands secondary sanctions targeting Chinese companies doing business with Iran, according to reporting by the Financial Times.The warning came after the Trump administration on Monday announced new sanctions targeting companies in Hong Kong and mainland China as part of a broader crackdown on Iran's oil trade. The measures, however, did not target major Chinese financial institutions, FT reported.A Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson said on Tuesday that Beijing would take "all necessary measures" to safeguard its rights and interests, while reiterating China's opposition to what it described as unilateral sanctions lacking a basis in international law or United Nations Security Council authorisation.The spokesperson also called for de-escalation and a return to dialogue and negotiations.China is Iran's largest oil customer, with Chinese buyers accounting for about 90% of Iran's oil exports. While China's state-owned refiners largely comply with US sanctions on Iranian crude, smaller private refiners, known as "teapots", continue to import and process Iranian oil.The United States has sanctioned some of those refiners this year, but Beijing has instructed Chinese companies not to comply with such measures.The prospect of broader US secondary sanctions comes at a sensitive point in US-China relations. US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping are expected to meet in Washington next month, with the leaders expected to discuss extending a one-year truce in their trade dispute.Wang Dong, a senior scholar at Peking University, told FT that Beijing would defend its national interests if Washington moved ahead with broader sanctions, though he said it remained unclear whether the US threat was serious or a negotiating tactic.Zhu Feng, an international relations expert at Nanjing University, told the newspaper that China was also keen to see the conflict between the United States and Iran de-escalate.Zhu said continued restrictions on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz were putting additional pressure on the global economy and could pose a greater risk to China because of its dependence on Middle Eastern oil supplies."From a Chinese perspective, we don't want to get involved in a US-Iranian fight," Zhu told FT.Chinese officials have also stepped up diplomatic contacts in the Middle East.
