[a1] Al Jazeera <https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/1/eritrea-severs-diplomatic-ties-with-ethiopia-in-tit-for-tat-move?traffic_source=rss> (FULL TEXT)
Eritrea says it is breaking diplomatic ties with its neighbour Ethiopia in response to the expulsion of diplomats from Addis Ababa, as fighting between Ethiopia’s federal forces and a coalition of armed opposition groups intensifies.

“Eritrea has no option but to reciprocate in kind and sever all diplomatic ties with Ethiopia with all the negative corollaries that this entails,” its Ministry of Foreign Affairs said in a statement on X on Thursday.

This came shortly after Ethiopia announced it would close its embassy in Asmara “in response to Eritrea’s direct and indirect acts of hostility and belligerence against Ethiopia”.

Ethiopia’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs also declared 10 Eritrean diplomats in Addis Ababa, including the charge d’affaires and others accredited to the African Union, “persona non grata for involvement in activities that pose a direct threat to Ethiopia’s national security”, requiring them to leave the country within 48 hours.

In its statement, Eritrea’s ministry said claims of its involvement in hostile activities were “astounding”.

“In reality, it is Ethiopia which has been pursuing an agenda of incessant hostility,” it said, adding that Addis Ababa had waged war against Eritrea to gain access to the Red Sea.

It also accused Ethiopia of hosting, training and arming Eritrean opposition groups to stoke internal conflict.

Addis Ababa has in turn accused Eritrea, ⁠Sudan and Egypt of supporting an opposition alliance led by the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF) that has fought against troops loyal to Ethiopia’s federal government since last week.

Fierce clashes between Ethiopian federal forces and the armed opposition groups are continuing. On Wednesday and Thursday, explosions were heard in Addis Ababa, while fighters have also clashed with government forces in the northern Afar region.

Days after the latest clashes broke out last week, the chief administrator of the Afar region, Haji Awol ⁠Arba, told Al Jazeera that Eritrean soldiers had crossed into Afar to join the alliance’s offensive.

The fighting marks the biggest escalation since the end of a 2020-2022 civil war. Eritrea fought alongside the Ethiopian government during the Tigray war, but relations between the neighbours have since deteriorated.

Eritrea was not a party to the Pretoria Peace Agreement that ended the fighting in November 2022, and ⁠relations between the two nations quickly soured.

Ties between the neighbours have been historically fragile. In 1962, Ethiopia annexed Eritrea. An armed independence struggle, led by Eritrea’s current President Isaias Afwerki, secured independence in 1993.

When Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed came to power in 2018, he moved to end the tensions and normalise diplomatic relations with President Isaias. But Abiy’s ambitions to seek direct sea access for landlocked Ethiopia have contributed to tensions.

In recent years, Abiy has repeated that Ethiopia has a right to sea access, words that officials in Asmara take as a declaration of territorial conflict with Eritrea.

[a2] DW World <https://www.dw.com/en/ethiopia-who-could-bring-the-warring-sides-to-the-negotiation-table/a-79475529?maca=en-rss-en-world-4025-rdf> (FULL TEXT)
Just four years after a landmark peace deal officially halted the devastating Tigray war, fighting has escalated once again in northern Ethiopia in recent weeks.

Just days before full-scale fighting resumed, several armed groups which had previously considered each enemies formally announced an alliance with a common goal: To bring down Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's federal government.

But this time, the lay of the land looks quite different from the previous conflict: Parties outside the country have been accused of meddling in the situation, with each having their own motives to want to destabilize Abiy's government.

This has raised fears about prolonged instability hitting the Horn of Africa.

The 2022 Pretoria Peace Agreement ended the conflict between the Ethiopian federal government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF), which cost hundreds of thousands of lives in the course of two years.

Negash Mohammed, a journalist with DW's Amharic department. believes that the deal collapsed for three key reasons — or rather because three key actors in the conflict were largely neglected at the negotiation table.

Mohammed explained that the ultimate agreement didn't factor in the role of Eritrea in the conflict, which had fought alongside Ethiopia against the TPLF; it ignored the part that Amhara militias — known as Fano — played in the war, who later felt sorely excluded from the negotiations; finally, it ignored harliners within the TPLF who viewed the peace peace as nothing but military defeat.

Tensions between the federal government and the Fano escalated further when Addis Ababa moved to forcibly disarm the militias instead of addressing their grievances.

"The federal government has its own shortcomings," said Mohammed. "Instead of convincing the other side, it went on to aggravate the grievances they [already] had."

Mohammed also emphasized the fact that this coalition against the federal government includes several groups which have otherwise little to do with each other.

It includes the Oromo Liberation Army, a faction of the Ogaden National Liberation Front as well as multiple smaller armed groups from the western Benishangul-Gumuz region — in addition to ethnic militant factions from Afar, which borders Tigray.

Eritrea meanwhile appears to have shifted sides in the current situation: While not officially a member of the alliance, the neighboring country is believed to be providing support to the rebels.

Patricia Rodrigues, a senior analyst at Control Risks, a global intelligence and security consultancy, referred to the level of strategic coordination among these various groups in the current conflict as "quite significant" and "definitely alarming for the federal government."

She noted, however, that cracks might eventually appear: "How long this coalition lasts, because all of them have very different interests and represent very different ethnic constituencies, remains to be seen," she told DW.

Rodrigues added that, based on Control Risks' assessment, the fighting is likely to remain limited to Ethiopia's northern regions, particularly those situated along the borders between Tigray, Amhara, and Afar.

Furthermore, she highlighted that the idea of it spreading across the entire country was rather unlikely due to the difficulty of nationwide coordination, taking into consideration the rough terrain of the nation and the fragmentation of other opposition groups.

Rodrigues also pointed out the failure of sustained international engagement after the end of the crisis in 2022 as a key reason for the current conflict_

She noted that while the Pretoria agreement saw some early proof of implementation — including the TPLF's surrender of heavy weaponry in 2022 and 2023 — international attention had soon shifted to other global crises, leaving both sides free to quietly rearm and conscript supporters.

"It was more of a stopgap or a ceasefire, but people were quickly rearming in the background," she told DW.

Rodrigues also noted that the United States had recently lifted sanctions imposed during the 2020–2022 conflict — a step which, according to Mohammed, diminishes the West's leverage in the conflict to push combatants toward coming to the negotiation table.

In the meantime, civilians are bearing the cost of the warring parties: People caught up in the crossfire have been fleeing the conflict once more, adding further to a population of internally displaced persons (IDPs) that has not recovered from the previous war.

"Since 2020, they have not returned to their areas. They are still in makeshift IDP centers," Mohammed said, adding that there have also been isolated reports of drone strikes hitting civilian areas and of rebel groups deploying military hardware inside schools and clinics.

Explosions were heard overnight in Ethiopia's capital, some in the area of the military headquarters in the west and another in the south of the city, residents and diplomatic sources say.

The blasts came hours after drone flights were banned over Addis Ababa and a week after fighting erupted in the north between the army and a newly launched alliance of armed groups including forces from the Tigray region.

A source linked to the opposition fighters told the Reuters news agency they were behind what they said were drone strikes.

"It was very loud. It was strong enough to set off car alarms," a resident in a southern area of the city, near the base of the secret service, told BBC Amharic.

He estimated that he heard the explosion in the Gotera neighbourhood some time around 00:30 local time (21:30 GMT).

Another resident told the BBC that he and his friends were playing games when they heard an explosion.

He lives in the western suburb of Torhayloch, a neighbourhood close to the military headquarters.

There were no official reports confirming the cause of the explosions, the exact locations affected or whether there were any casualties or damage.

The office of Ethiopia's Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, the defence ministry as well as the federal and Addis Ababa police have not responded to BBC requests for comment.

Meanwhile, Ethiopia's foreign ministry has said it is closing its embassy in neighbouring Eritrea and expelling 10 Eritrean diplomats "for involvement in activities that pose a direct threat to Ethiopia's national security".

Earlier, in a BBC interview, Eritrea's government denied that it was backing the new rebel alliance.

Clashes broke out last week after months of tension between the government and the party that controls Tigray, the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF).

They both accuse each other of violating a 2022 peace deal that ended a brutal two-year civil war, which according to an Africa Union mediator is estimated to have cost 600,000 lives.

Last month, seven armed movements, including the TPLF and an Amhara militia known as Fano, formed an alliance with the stated aim of removing the government in Addis Ababa.

There is disagreement over which side started the latest fighting.

The TPLF says it was responding to a federal offensive, including drone strikes, while the government says Tigrayan forces attacked first after seizing control of airports in the region.

The BBC is unable to report from Tigray and surrounding areas.

It is difficult to verify what is happening on the ground and access to the internet and the main telecoms service provider in Tigray have also been cut.

Within Tigray, the national army and a pro-government militia appear to be advancing towards the region's main city of Mekelle.

In the southern Tigrayan town of Alamata, a medic told the BBC on Wednesday that 52 civilians had died in three days of heavy fighting.

The TPLF is also reported to be fighting in neighbouring Afar region, a strategic area which connects landlocked Ethiopia to Djibouti and its ports.

A humanitarian source has estimated that tens of thousands have fled their homes in Afar, according to the AFP news agency.

Fighting around Lalibela in the Amhara region, a Unesco world heritage site where foreign tourists have been stranded for more than a week, has involved the national army and Fano fighters, now allied to the TPLF.

The government has accused neighbouring Eritrea of backing these groups.

One pro-government militia commander fighting in Tigray told the BBC that Eritrean soldiers were involved on the front lines.

But in a rare response to the BBC from Eritrea's government, Information Minister Yemane Gebremeskel dismissed the allegations.

He accused Ethiopia's ruling Prosperity Party (PP) of seeking external scapegoats for the country's mounting challenges.

"The PP regime has made it a habit to apportion blame to its neighbours in order to deflect attention from the myriad internal conflicts that it is fomenting in various parts of the country," he told BBC Tigrinya.

Eritrea was a key ally of Ethiopia during the Tigray war, fighting against the TPLF.

But relations between the two neighbours have since deteriorated largely over the Ethiopian prime minister's ambition to take ownership of one of Eritrea's Red Sea ports.

Abiy has even hinted at taking the port of Assab - about 60km (37 miles) from the border - by force as he sees direct access to the sea as important for Ethiopia's long-term future and economic prosperity.

As concerns over security mount in Ethiopia, the country's National Intelligence and Security Service (Niss) announced on Wednesday that it was imposing an indefinite ban on drone flights in Addis Ababa and surrounding areas.

The agency said it would not issue permits for drone operations and warned that legal action would be taken against anyone flying drones in restricted areas.

Fires have broken out at several schools in France, as students protest by blockading education institutions nationwide over school funding and studying conditions.

Exports of crude from the strait of Hormuz have largely returned to levels seen before the outbreak of the Iran war, as oil producers and the shipping industry have found alternative ways of transporting crucial fuel out of the Middle East.

Pipeline exports and ship-to-ship transfers are among the methods being used, according to analysts tracking the situation, while the US military continues to escort some vessels. However, flows of refined products such as diesel remain constrained, pushing prices higher.

At least 16.5m barrels per day (bpd) left the region in September, according to figures from the global trade intelligence firm Kpler, equalling the pre-war average, excluding Iran. The figure is 10.5m bpd higher than the monthly average for March, during the first weeks of the Iran war.

Since the conflict began on 28 February with US and Israeli strikes on Iran, Tehran has sought to exert its authority on the strait of Hormuz and has at times even declared the strategic waterway closed.

However, Iran’s control appears to have declined in recent weeks, as oil exporters have found workarounds to address the disruption.

About 40% of the region’s crude is now transported without transiting the strait, compared with just 17% before the war, through Saudi and Emirati pipelines, according to Kpler analysis. Saudi Arabia was able to restart operations on its east-west pipeline in late September, after it was damaged during drone attacks, allowing exports from resume from the Red Sea port of Yanbu.

In a sign of the other adaptations, the majority of crude transported through the strait of Hormuz is carried on a shuttle fleet mostly comprising very large crude carriers, sailing with their satellite transponders turned off. The cargo is moved on to different tankers in open water, usually off the coast of Oman or Fujairah in the United Arab Emirates.

More than 70% of the crude that passed through the strait in August changed tankers, whereas before the war almost no Gulf crude changed ships in the Gulf of Oman.

However, the recovery in exports from the region remains unbalanced, and supplies of refined products remain constrained.

This poses a problem for businesses and households that depend on refined products such as diesel to run their cars, vans and lorries.
