Ministers discuss drawing down EU countries’ reserves after Donald Trump threatens to cut off US supplies

Britain is in talks with European allies over releasing emergency diesel stockpiles after Donald Trump threatened to cut off US supplies of the fuel.

Ministers held calls with counterparts from the European Commission (EC), Germany, France, Italy and Ireland on Thursday to discuss whether to draw down reserves after the Trump administration told Germany and France to release their stockpiles to help ease soaring global energy prices or face a US export ban.

The warning marks an escalation of pressure on Europe as Trump considers a potential wider ban to help lower domestic fuel prices before the US midterm elections in November.

Trump said Washington could ask its European allies to release strategic diesel reserves. “We may do that. They have some diesel,” Trump told reporters on a midterm elections campaign trip to Texas, shortly after the Treasury secretary, Scott Bessent, urged Europe to “immediately” release its reserves.

Bessent flagged the potential diesel cuts in a social media post, writing: “Our European partners should accelerate delivery on their existing commitments and make additional supplies immediately available to address ongoing disruptions.”

Martin McCluskey, the UK minister for local energy, was reportedly on the call with EU countries on Thursday.

The Trump administration has been frustrated with France and Germany for what it considers their not following through on commitments to release oil and petrol stocks.

Fuel stations across Europe are unlikely to run dry, because Europe produces about 70% of the diesel it consumes from domestic refineries. It also stores fuels in reserve. But experts fear that competing for cargoes on the global market would lead to higher market prices.

It was unclear whether the UK would be included in such a ban, but the loss of US supply would drive prices up sharply at the pumps because the UK does not refine enough diesel to meet domestic demand. About a third of UK diesel imports last year came from the US.

The average price of a litre of diesel in the UK hit a new all-time high on Wednesday of 199.72p, according to the motoring body the RAC.

John Healey, the chancellor, admitted earlier this week that UK diesel prices were “extreme” and said the government was in discussion with the US over trying to stop a ban.

EC commission spokesperson Anna Kaisa Itkonen confirmed that EU member states will hold urgent talks with the commission on Friday to discuss a coordinated response.

“The commission … is closely coordinating with EU member states to take stock of the situation and examine appropriate measures to tackle these high prices,” Itkonen added. Discussions will also involve the International Energy Agency.

The US war in Iran has disrupted energy supplies via the strait of Hormuz and sent the price of oil soaring. Before the war, in February, Brent crude – the international benchmark for oil prices – traded at about $72 a barrel. It was above $101 on Thursday.

Releasing more stocks would represent a problem for European countries, which need to balance the need to bring down domestic fuel prices with keeping high stocks for a potential worsening energy crisis over the winter if the Iran war continues.

“The UK continues to need fuels – from transport to chemicals, agriculture and defence,” said Elizabeth de Jong, the chief executive of the trade group Fuels Industry UK. The latest US threat showed the UK “cannot solely rely on overseas production for domestic energy security”, she added.

Trump has said he was “very seriously” considering a US diesel export ban to try to curb soaring prices, and has urged Ukraine’s president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, to pause strikes on Russian oil refineries over concerns that they could further push up prices. Russia had already extended its ban on diesel exports until the end of October after Ukrainian attacks on refining companies.

A government spokesperson said: “We have a diverse and resilient supply. We continue to engage with our international partners and the UK fuel industry.”
