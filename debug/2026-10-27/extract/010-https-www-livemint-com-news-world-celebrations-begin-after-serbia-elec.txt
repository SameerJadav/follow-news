Serbia witnessed nationwide celebrations on Monday after a student-backed movement defeated longtime leader Aleksandar Vucic's ruling alliance in a landmark parliamentary election, ending 14 years of political dominance and setting the stage for a major shift in the Balkan country's leadership.

Tens of thousands of people took to the streets of Belgrade and other Serbian cities after Vucic conceded defeat late on Sunday, according to Reuters. The result followed nearly two years of peaceful, student-led anti-government protests demanding accountability and calling for the populist leader to step down.

With almost 99% of votes counted, the student-backed candidates had secured 52.7% of the vote, compared with 39.2% for the alliance led by Vucic's Serbian Progressive Party (SNS), according to the State Election Commission, Reuters reported.

The student-backed list won 139 seats in Serbia's 250-member parliament, while the SNS-led alliance secured 97. Minority parties won the remaining seats.

The Associated Press (AP) described the outcome as a historic change of government, following a grassroots movement that mobilised university students against corruption, cronyism and organised crime.

However, while the election result prompted celebrations among supporters, uncertainty remains over who will lead the incoming government and how the diverse movement will translate its protest demands into policy.

The movement gained momentum in 2024 after a canopy collapsed at a renovated railway station in Novi Sad, northern Serbia, killing 16 people. The tragedy triggered allegations that corruption and negligence had contributed to poor construction work, according to AP.

Students led protests demanding accountability and action against corruption. Over nearly two years, the demonstrations developed into a wider challenge to Vucic's government, with the movement attracting support from people frustrated with the state of public institutions.

Reuters reported that the student-backed candidates included university professors, actors and farmers. Rather than emerging from a conventional political party with an established leadership structure, the movement grew out of the protests and campaigned on an agenda centred on tackling corruption and improving education.

Its message resonated with voters, AP reported, as the movement challenged a ruling party that had dominated Serbian politics for more than a decade.

Vucic and the SNS repeatedly denied accusations of corruption and repression.

Vucic attempted to strengthen his position ahead of the election by resigning from the presidency to pursue a bid to become prime minister. He also launched a campaign that included favourable coverage on state media, attacks on political opponents and promises of higher pensions, new roads and cheaper medicines.

Those efforts failed to prevent the student-backed candidates from securing a decisive victory.

The movement's criticism of corruption, cronyism and organised crime under Vucic resonated widely with voters.

The election result also came amid longstanding concerns about the fairness of elections under Vucic. There were allegations of voting irregularities, violent incidents, favourable treatment in pro-government media and the use of public funds to secure electoral support. Vucic's government and party have faced repeated criticism over these issues, while the ruling side has denied accusations of wrongdoing.

After conceding defeat on Sunday, Vucic blamed foreign interference.

"I want people to know," he said. "This was the verification of a revolution that they carried out directly with support of different European countries and the deep American state, the one that is also against President (Donald) Trump."

"Our structure is very firm, we are very united and strong, and we are used to being in opposition," Vucic said in a Sunday night speech, according to AP.

Former Serbian President Boris Tadic said Vucic might not have conceded had the student-backed movement secured a less convincing victory. Under the circumstances, however, Tadic said, "he could not avoid it."

Vucic has been one of the most influential figures in Serbian politics for more than a decade. According to AP, he served as deputy prime minister, prime minister and president over the past 14 years, turning the largely ceremonial presidency into the centre of political power.

His political career spans more than 30 years. AP reported that Vucic began as an ultranationalist who advocated the creation of a “Greater Serbia” during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s before later shifting towards a declared pro-European position.

As information minister in the late 1990s, he oversaw a period in which media outlets critical of then-President Slobodan Milosevic faced heavy fines or closure, AP reported.
