Trump says press secretary Karoline Leavitt will leave role to spend more time with family

Karoline Leavitt, the White House press secretary, will leave her role at the end of this month to spend more time with her family, including her two young children, Donald Trump announced today.

The president said on social media that it was a “decision I totally understand and respect”, while adding that Leavitt would become one of his “top outside advisors” when she leaves the administration.

Leavitt was 27 when she assumed the role of press secretary at the beginning of Trump’s second term. She is the youngest person in US history to hold the role. She was previously a press aide during the first Trump administration, before launching an unsuccessful bid for a US House seat in New Hampshire, her home state.

“Karoline has been a real leader in the White House, and has done a phenomenal job fighting for Justice, Liberty, and Freedom, since 2018,” Trump wrote on Truth Social.

Leavitt gave birth to her second child, a daughter, in May of this year, and recently returned from parental leave.

In her first press briefing, Karoline Leavitt promised to never lie, and then told a wildly false story about the US sending condoms to Gaza

When Karoline Leavitt appeared in the White House briefing room for the first time as press secretary, on 28 January 2025, she adhered to what was then a longstanding tradition and called on the Associated Press to ask the first question.

Zeke Miller, then the AP’s White House correspondent, asked Leavitt what he called “a question that we’ve asked your predecessors of both parties in this job”.

“When you’re up here in this briefing room speaking to the American public, do you view yourself and your role as speaking on, advocating on behalf of the president, or providing the unvarnished truth? That is, you know, not to lie, not to obfuscate to the American people?”

“I commit to telling the truth from this podium every single day,” Leavitt replied. “I would like to point out, while I vow to provide the truth from this podium, we ask that all of you in this room hold yourselves to that same standard.

“We know for a fact there have been lies that have been pushed by many legacy media outlets in this country about this president, about his family, and we will not accept that,” she went on. “We will call you out when we feel that your reporting is wrong or there is misinformation about this White House.

“So, yes, I will hold myself to the truth, and I expect everyone in this room to do the same.”

Just five minutes later, Leavitt told the assembled reporters, and the American people, a wildly false story to justify Trump’s sweeping order to freeze federal funding by citing what she called important new research from Elon Musk’s “department of government efficiency” (or Doge), and the office of management and budget (OMB).

“Doge and OMB also found that there was about to be 50m taxpayer dollars that went out the door to fund condoms in Gaza,” Leavitt claimed. “That is a preposterous waste of taxpayer money. So that’s what this pause is focused on: being good stewards of tax dollars”.

While Leavitt did not offer any evidence to support this claim, and was not pressed to by reporters in the room, the idea that the United States government had budgeted $50m to send condoms to Gaza quickly went viral, with an assist from Musk and the Fox pundit Jesse Watters, who claimed that day that the condoms were being used by Hamas militants as balloons to float explosives into Israel.

However, as the Guardian reported later that day, a comprehensive report issued in September 2025 by the US Agency for International Development (USAID), showed that not a penny of the $60.8m in contraceptive and condom shipments funded by the US in the past year went to Gaza. In fact, no condoms were sent to any part of the Middle East, and just one small shipment, $45,680 in oral and injectable contraceptives, had been sent to the region, all of it distributed to the government of Jordan.

No USAID funding for family planning was budgeted for Gaza.

The USAID report was quickly removed from the government website where it had been posted (but not before it was archived) and the administration never provided any evidence for the claim, even after Trump boasted a day later that his administration had “stopped $50m being sent to Gaza to buy condoms for Hamas”.

Experts on US aid to Gaza were baffled by the claim. “We have asked around, and no one is sure what this is referring to,” Steve Fake, a spokesperson for Anera, a non-profit that has partnered with USAID on a five-year, $50m health initiative in Gaza, told CNN at the time. Anera’s program, he added, has “definitely no purchase of condoms”.

The International Medical Corps – a US-based organization that operated two field hospitals in Gaza with US support – also posted a statement on its website detailing its emergency work in Gaza with this clear denial: “No US government funding was used to procure or distribute condoms.”

At a subsequent briefing two weeks later, Leavitt defended the White House decision to bar the Associated Press from attending briefings by accusing the news agency of telling “lies” by refusing to refer to the Gulf of Mexico as the “Gulf of America” after Trump claimed the power to unilaterally change the name of the international body of water.

Karoline Leavitt signs off as White House press secretary with a partisan attack on Democrats, in apparent violation of the law

In a long social media post confirming her departure from the role of White House press secretary at the end of August, Karoline Leavitt promised to remain a “a vocal advocate for MAGA and the Republican Party” and included a direct, partisan attack on the Democratic party. Both parts of the statement would appear to violate the Hatch Act, the federal law that bars federal government employees from engaging in partisan political activity or disparaging a political opponent while on duty.

While Leavitt posted her statement on a personal social media account, it was then shared on the official government account for the press secretary, which still bears her name and image, and by two official White House social media accounts.

In 2019, the US office of special counsel, which is responsible for enforcing the Hatch Act, asked then president Donald Trump to fire his White House adviser Kellyanne Conway for “repeatedly violat[ing] the Hatch Act during her official media appearances by making statements directed at the success of your reelection campaign or at the failure of candidates for the Democratic Party’s nomination for President”.

Trump ignored the recommendation and his second term in office has been marked by highly partisan statements from government officials and the social media accounts and websites of multiple federal government agencies.

Early in Trump’s second term he fired the office of special counsel’s leader, Hampton Dellinger, who was eventually ousted after a protracted legal battle.

Two weeks ago, the office of special counsel posted this clear warning to federal employees about their social media activity while at work: “Don’t share or repost content that supports or opposes a political candidate, party or group.”

Here is the section of Leavitt’s statement that might have triggered an investigation by the office of special counsel in a previous administration:

Being a mother and welcoming a new baby while working in one of the most demanding jobs in the world has been the most rewarding yet challenging season of my life, to say the least. The truth is since returning to the White House after the birth of my daughter, I have felt in my heart that I cannot be the best mom my two young children deserve while devoting the constant time, energy, and attention required of the White House Press Secretary – and that is why I have ultimately made the bittersweet decision to depart the White House and embark on a new chapter in my life.

President Trump has asked me to continue serving as a top advisor to him on the outside, and I will always remain a vocal advocate for MAGA and the Republican Party.

Our country is facing an existential threat from an increasingly extremist Democrat Party that seeks to destroy everything great about America, and I believe it is incumbent upon all of us who care about this country to fight back against that threat. My fight is entering a new phase, but it is far from over.

Leavitt's time as press secretary - at a glance

Throughout her time in the White House, Leavitt has been a fixture of the administration.

  • She often had a combative relationship with members of the press during her regular White House briefings. There were often moments where she derided members of the mainstream media, whom she accused of bias. For example, earlier this year she called Niall Stanage, a reporter from the Hill, a “leftwing hack” after he pressed Leavitt on the killing of Renee Good by an Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agent in January. In her social media post announcing her decision to resign, Leavitt said that she “relished holding the liberal media accountable and ensuring the American people hear the truth about President Trump’s successes”.

  • Her time as press secretary saw an influx of rightwing media pundits in the briefing room. Leavitt also attempted to take control over which outlets would serve in the White House press pool – a group of dedicated outlets that cover the president’s daily movements. The White House Correspondents’ Association – an independent body that manages the pool – rejected the proposal. Leavitt and her team have introduced a “new media” seat at the White House – which often includes conservative commentators who are friendly with the Trump administration. Leavitt was also a defendant in the Associated Press lawsuit, which saw her press team block the AP from serving in the press pool in retaliation for the outlet refusing to refer to the Gulf of Mexico as the “Gulf of America”.

  • Leavitt became one of the president’s most loyal foot soldiers. During his first term, Donald Trump cycled through five press secretaries. His first, Sean Spicer, left the White House after six months. Leavitt, however, proved a key ally for Trump – often receiving praise. However, she is not the president’s longest-serving press secretary. Sarah Huckabee Sanders served in the role for almost two years in the first Trump White House.

  • Leavitt achieved a number of firsts while in office. At 27, she was the youngest press secretary in US history. And when she announced she was pregnant in December last year, she became the first ever expectant press secretary.

  • However, Leavitt had to postpone her maternity leave after the White House correspondents’ dinner shooting. Leavitt delayed her leave to deliver the administration’s first on‑camera response after the shooting in April.

Trump sued over $100,000-a-month early-access offer to Truth Social posts

Joseph Gedeon

Donald Trump has been sued after a subscription service charging up to $100,000 a month for early access to his posts on his Truth Social platform sparked allegations of corruption.

The lawsuit, filed in the US district court for the southern district of New York, was brought by the Intercept and the Freedom of the Press Foundation against Trump, two of his aides and the executive office of the president.

It targets “Truth API”, a paid data feed launched on 1 August by the Trump Media & Technology Group, the company behind Truth Social. The complaint calls the arrangement “extraordinary, corrupt and unconstitutional”.

The service gives paying subscribers faster access than the general public to posts from 10 of the platform’s highest-profile accounts, including Trump’s own account, as well as those of the vice-president, JD Vance; press secretary Karoline Leavitt; and other senior officials. Subscribers pay $100,000 a month, or $60,000 a month for a three-year commitment.

The White House and Trump Media did not immediately respond to requests for comment on the lawsuit.

Trump has a history of using Truth Social to disclose market-sensitive news, including posts on tariffs and the war on Iran, which have triggered swings in stock and oil prices. Trump Media’s interim chief executive, Kevin McGurn, has even promoted the service as a way to give businesses fast access to the platform’s “most market-moving” posts. He has described it as a “high-margin” product meant to provide a “meaningful, ongoing source of revenue”.

Close aides but no cabinet officials joined Trump in Air Force One ruse – report

Earlier this week, astonishing reports emerged around Donald Trump being secretly spirited off an Air Force One jet on a catering truck and into a different plane in response to a potential Iranian assassination threat last month in Turkey.

Democrats have called for an immediate congressional hearing into the ruse, and critics have raised serious questions over why reporters and the public had been deliberately misled. According to my colleague Martin Belam in this morning’s First Thing newsletter, more than a hundred aides, officials and reporters were kept onboard, without being told there was a threat to their lives.

“It appears that the idea was we can reduce the danger to President Trump by offering up 100 or so people to be killed by the Iranians in his place,” said Steven Cash, executive director of the Steady State and a former CIA intelligence officer and senior adviser to the Department of Homeland Security’s intelligence branch.

Today, the Washington Post (paywall) further reports that the president was accompanied by some of his most loyal and longest-serving aides – but no cabinet officials.

Other passengers on the truck included Dan Scavino, the White House deputy chief of staff; Natalie Harp, the president’s executive assistant; and Walt Nauta, the director of Oval Office operations, according to a US official familiar with the operation and corroborating material reviewed by the paper, which first reported the story on Monday.

According to the Post’s report, a few lower-profile aides were also present, but the US secretary of state, Marco Rubio, and other officials at the highest level of the government were not. Indeed, the story goes:

Other senior administration officials, military personnel, Secret Service agents and journalists remained on the legacy Air Force One plane, which had been deemed too risky for the president’s travel.

It remains unclear whether all the top administration officials involved were aware they were flying on a different plane than the president or whether they, like the reporters assigned to be in the pool covering him, were kept in the dark.

In his first public comments about the operation, Trump told reporters last night at Joint Base Andrews that the decision for him to take an alternative flight was made by the Secret Service.

“The plane that I was on was at greater risk,” he said. “Because that would be the plane that I think that they would be more likely to go for.”

Richard Blumenthal, a Democratic senator for Connecticut, called the operation “unprecedented and surreal” and “downright scary” on CNN yesterday. “There has to be a congressional briefing because it involves not just the safety of the president.

“I want to know what precautions were taken to make sure that all of the passengers on that Air Force One, which was judged too dangerous for the president to fly on, what precautions were taken to keep the staff and reporters on that plane safe?”

Mike Lindell refuses to concede in Minnesota primary despite getting far fewer votes

Joseph Gedeon

Mike Lindell, the pillow salesman, Trump supporter and election denier, has refused to concede defeat in Minnesota’s Republican gubernatorial primary, despite trailing by a double-digit margin the morning after polls closed.

The Associated Press called the race for Lisa Demuth, the Minnesota House speaker, late last night. With almost all votes counted, Demuth was more than 45,000 votes ahead of Lindell, who had the backing of Donald Trump, beating him by 43.4% to 32.5%. Businessman Kendall Qualls, backed by the Republican state party, was trailing in third.

Told by a reporter on his own streaming channel, LindellTV, that the AP had declared the race over, Lindell dismissed the wire service outright. Asked why he wouldn’t accept the call, he questioned the AP’s authority to determine the outcome and insisted that hundreds of thousands of votes remained uncounted.

“I’m not going to give up this race until the votes are counted,” Lindell said. “That’s nonsense.”

Lindell did not return a request for comment on what steps he will take next to contest the election, as he waits for the votes to come in.

Lindell built his political career on the false claim that Trump’s 2020 defeat was the product of a rigged and stolen election, spending what he has said were tens of millions of dollars pursuing that conspiracy theory through lawsuits, documentaries and conferences, even as courts repeatedly threw it out. Last month he settled a $1.3bn defamation suit brought by voting technology company Liberty Vote, formerly known as Dominion Voting Systems, regarding the stolen election lies.

Lindell’s campaign was also dogged by questions over his own eligibility to run. He was, for weeks, not registered to vote in the state whose governorship he was seeking – his registration remained active in Texas, tied to a property owned by a company in Lufkin, even as he told reporters he had re-established Minnesota residency. Minnesota’s same-day registration rules meant he could still cast a ballot, and run, regardless.

Trump, who has failed to carry Minnesota in three consecutive presidential elections, made a late, personal push for Lindell on Tuesday, urging Minnesota Republicans on social media to hand him a decisive win and praising his commitment to “free and fair” elections.

Lindell’s defeat extended a run of losses for Trump-endorsed candidates, including House races for Andy Ogles, a representative in Tennessee, and Amir Hassan, a navy veteran in Michigan who lost to a candidate who had dropped out.

Trump administration again urges supreme court intervention on mail-in ballots

Donald Trump’s administration has again asked the US supreme court to intervene to fully ⁠implement his executive ⁠order restricting the ​use of mail-in ballots ahead of November’s midterm elections that will decide control of Congress.

The justice department filed a request today asking the ⁠justices to put on hold a decision made yesterday by the US district judge Indira Talwani in Boston that blocks the US Postal Service from nationwide enforcement ⁠of the directive’s tighter rules for voting by mail.

Trump signed his executive order in March after ​years of calling for restrictions on ‌voting by mail and pushing false claims that his 2020 election defeat was the result of widespread voter fraud. A reminder that he has never provided evidence for that and has voted by mail himself.

Talwani’s decision yesterday, in a case brought by several voting rights groups and represented by the American Civil Liberties Union, effectively expands on an earlier order she issued in June preventing the enforcement of Trump’s order in 23 mostly Democratic-governed states and Washington DC, which had also challenged the action as unconstitutional.

The administration has already asked the supreme court to pause the June decision. In ‌its brief filing today, the justice department urged the court to lift that decision and make clear that the ruling applies to the judge’s ​action yesterday as well.

The administration contends that the lawsuits are premature as federal agencies have not yet taken any concrete actions that affect the plaintiffs, and so any harm they allege is speculative.

Trump’s executive order directed the Department of Homeland Security to compile and transmit to the states a list of ⁠US citizens eligible to vote in each state, and the justice department to prioritize ​investigating and prosecuting state ​and local election officials who issue ballots ​to people deemed “not eligible” to vote in federal elections.

It also required the ​Postal Service to ‌deliver ballots only to voters ​on each state’s ​approved mail-in ballot list. The Postal Service recently moved to implement Trump’s directive.

In siding with the voting rights groups yesterday, Talwani wrote that the executive branch of the federal government had no authority to regulate elections, a responsibility that the US constitution assigns to individual states.
