Can anyone broker a new peace between Tigray and Ethiopia?

Eddy Micah Jr. | Silja Fröhlich
10/02/2026October 2, 2026

Ethiopia might be facing its most complex armed conflict in contemporary times. The fragile 2022 peace deal has collapsed. International mediators have failed. How will the country return to normal?

Just four years after a landmark peace deal officially halted the devastating Tigray war, fighting has escalated once again in northern Ethiopia in recent weeks. 

Just days before full-scale fighting resumed, several armed groups which had previously considered each enemies formally announced an alliance with a common goal: To bring down Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's federal government.

But this time, the situation looks quite different from the previous conflict: Parties outside the country have been accused of meddling in the situation, with each having their own motives to want to destabilize Abiy's government.

This has raised fears about prolonged instability hitting the Horn of Africa.

Reasons for the collapse of the peace deal

The 2022 Pretoria Peace Agreement ended the conflict between the Ethiopian federal government and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF), which cost hundreds of thousands of lives in the course of two years.

Negash Mohammed, a journalist with DW's Amharic department. believes that the deal collapsed for three key reasons — or rather because three key actors in the conflict were largely neglected at the negotiation table.

The final agreement didn't factor in the role of Eritrea in the conflict, which had fought alongside Ethiopia against the TPLF, Mohammed said. It also ignored the part that Amhara militias — known as Fano — played in the war, who later felt sorely excluded from the negotiations; finally, it ignored hardliners within the TPLF who viewed the peace as a military defeat.

Tensions between the federal government and the Fano escalated further when Abiy's government moved to forcibly disarm the militias instead of addressing their grievances.

"The federal government has its own shortcomings," said Mohammed. "Instead of convincing the other side, it went on to aggravate the grievances they [already] had."

An alliance of former enemies

Mohammed also emphasized that the coalition against the federal government includes several groups which have otherwise little to do with each other.

It includes the Oromo Liberation Army, a faction of the Ogaden National Liberation Front, as well as multiple smaller armed groups from the western Benishangul-Gumuz region and also ethnic militant factions from Afar, which borders Tigray.

Eritrea meanwhile appears to have shifted sides in the current situation: While not officially a member of the alliance, the neighboring country is believed to be providing support to the rebels.

The new rebel alliance transforming the Tigray conflict

Patricia Rodrigues, a senior analyst at Control Risks, a global intelligence and security consultancy, referred to the level of strategic coordination among these various groups in the current conflict as "quite significant" and "definitely alarming for the federal government."

She noted, however, that cracks might eventually appear: "How long this coalition lasts, because all of them have very different interests and represent very different ethnic constituencies, remains to be seen," she told DW.

Rodrigues added that, based on Control Risks' assessment, the fighting is likely to remain limited to Ethiopia's northern regions, particularly those situated along the borders between Tigray, Amhara and Afar. 

Furthermore, she highlighted that the idea of it spreading across the entire country was rather unlikely due to the difficulty of nationwide coordination, taking into consideration the rough terrain of the nation and the fragmentation of other opposition groups.

Could the conflict have been prevented?

Rodrigues also pointed to the failure of sustained international engagement after the end of the crisis in 2022 as a key reason for the current conflict.

She noted that while the Pretoria agreement saw some early proof of implementation — including the TPLF's surrender of heavy weaponry in 2022 and 2023 — international attention had soon shifted to other global crises, leaving both sides free to quietly rearm and conscript supporters.

"It was more of a stopgap or a ceasefire, but people were quickly rearming in the background," she told DW.

The United States had recently lifted sanctions imposed during the 2020–2022 conflict — a step which, according to Mohammed, diminishes the West's leverage in the conflict to push combatants to the negotiating table.

The real cost of war

In the meantime, civilians are bearing the cost of the warring parties: People caught up in the crossfire have been fleeing the conflict once more, adding further to a population of internally displaced persons (IDPs) that has not recovered from the previous war.

"Since 2020, they have not returned to their areas. They are still in makeshift IDP centers," Mohammed said, adding that there have also been isolated reports of drone strikes hitting civilian areas and of rebel groups deploying military hardware inside schools and clinics.

Telephone and internet connections have also been cut in parts of Tigray and Amhara, electricity has been disrupted, and flights have been suspended to several cities including Kombolcha and Lalibela.

Despite official assurances from all parties that civilians would be protected, Mohammed says that the reality on the ground tells a different story: "Words and practice are very contradictory."

Limited prospects for mediation

UN Secretary-General António Guterres dispatched a special envoy to the Horn of Africa, charged with mediating between the African Union, regional powers and Egypt. These are many of the same parties involved in brokering the Pretoria agreement.

But, according to Mohammed, meaningful negotiations are unlikely to bear fruit until at least one of the sides involved in the conflict "comes to understand that this violence will not help them to achieve what they want."

Rodrigues has a similar opinion, stressing that she cannot be optimistic about "an international or a regional mediation being the solution to the current fighting," adding that "intense, but geographically contained" fighting will continue for the foreseeable future.
