## News •

What began as a tongue-in-cheek swipe against remarks made by the chief [justice](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/justice) of the [Supreme Court of India](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Supreme-Court-of-India) snowballed into a [satirical](https://www.britannica.com/art/satire) political movement on [social media](https://www.britannica.com/topic/social-media) and then mobilized thousands of protestors on the ground demanding education reforms. The **Cockroach Janta Party** (CJP; “Cockroach People’s Party”) is not a registered [political party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/political-party) but a [caricature](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/caricature) of one. In just a matter of days, the CJP garnered more than 20 million followers on [Instagram](https://www.britannica.com/money/Instagram)—eclipsing those of the ruling [Bharatiya Janata Party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Bharatiya-Janata-Party) and the [Indian National Congress](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress), India’s biggest political entities. Though built on self-mockery and [Internet](https://www.britannica.com/technology/Internet)[memes](https://www.britannica.com/topic/meme), the movement posed potent questions to the political establishment and positioned itself as a mouthpiece for the frustrations of [India](https://www.britannica.com/place/India)’s [Gen Z](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Generation-Z), resulting in one of the biggest protest movements in India in more than a decade. (_See also_: [Generation Z protests](https://www.britannica.com/event/Generation-Z-protests).)

## How did the Cockroach Janta Party start?

CJP’s [website](https://www.britannica.com/technology/website) and social media handles were created on May 16, 2026, by Abhijeet Dipke, a political communications strategist and [Boston University](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Boston-University) graduate who has previously worked with the [Aam Aadmi Party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Aam-Aadmi-Party) (AAP). The “cockroach” in CJP’s name references comments made by Chief Justice Surya Kant a day earlier in a Supreme Court hearing, in which he compared some youth to “[cockroaches](https://www.britannica.com/animal/cockroach-insect), who don’t get any employment or have any place in profession.” He also said that some of them become [journalists](https://www.britannica.com/topic/journalism), social media activists, or right-to-information campaigners and “start attacking everyone.” Though the top judge later clarified that his remarks related only to individuals with spurious [degrees](https://www.britannica.com/topic/degree-education) and not India’s youth in general, the remarks kicked up a social media storm—which fueled thousands of sign-ups on CJP’s website, taking even Dipke by surprise.

## Early activities

With the humble but [resilient](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/resilient) cockroach as its mascot, the CJP continues to rack up followers and share content on Instagram that touches on several topical issues in India, including [unemployment](https://www.britannica.com/money/unemployment), rising [fuel](https://www.britannica.com/science/fossil-fuel) prices, and delays in competitive [examinations](https://www.britannica.com/topic/examination-law). Several notable personalities and even politicians, including [Samajwadi Party](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Samajwadi-Party) supremo [Akhilesh Yadav](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Akhilesh-Yadav) and [Trinamool Congress](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Trinamool-Congress) members Mahua Moitra and Kirti Azad, have [endorsed](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/endorsed) the parody account and the associated movement. CJP’s website proclaims it is the “Voice of the Lazy& Unemployed” and features a mock [manifesto](https://www.britannica.com/topic/manifesto) that raises questions on disenfranchisement, [press freedom](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Press-Freedom-in-the-21st-Century), and controversial political practices, including the appointment of retired judges to [Parliament](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Parliament-of-India). All these half-serious demands notably echo [criticisms](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/criticisms) of and allegations against [Prime Minister](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Prime-Minister-of-India)[Narendra Modi](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Narendra-Modi)’s administration. However, critics of the CJP have insinuated that the movement may have been orchestrated by opposition parties, given Dipke’s past links to the AAP.

## Protests and parliament march

On June 1, 2026, Dipke announced CJP’s plans to take its campaign offline beyond social media, with a protest planned for June 6 at Jantar Mantar in [New Delhi](https://www.britannica.com/place/New-Delhi). Ahead of the event, journalist Saurav Das, whom CJP named its chief spokesperson, led a press conference at which the group said that the gathering would seek the resignation of central government education minister, Dharmendra Pradhan, over controversies surrounding qualifying examinations for medical studies as well as school board examinations. Dipke traveled to India from the [United States](https://www.britannica.com/place/United-States), where he resides, to lead the demonstration. A large number of supporters, mainly young people and students, gathered at the site on the day to demand Pradhan’s resignation, calling for him to take [moral](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/moral) responsibility for “paper leaks” related to the National Eligibility cum Entrance Test (NEET), a mandatory exam for admission to medical courses in the country. About 2 million students appear for the exam every year, competing for only about 130,000 seats. Some students invest in years of intense preparation for the exam. Days after NEET 2026 was conducted, the examination was canceled as reports swirled that questions from the exam had been secretly divulged to some students prior to the exam and other irregularities had been identified. With grueling preparation wasted and potential qualification dashed, at least a dozen NEET students died by [suicide](https://www.britannica.com/topic/suicide) in the following days, which became a major focus of CJP’s protest. Social activist [Sonam Wangchuk](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Sonam-Wangchuk), who had voiced his support for the CJP earlier and called himself an “honorary cockroach,” also attended the peaceful protest.

The CJP organized protests in other parts of the country, including [Pune](https://www.britannica.com/place/Pune), [Jaipur](https://www.britannica.com/place/Jaipur-India), [Lucknow](https://www.britannica.com/place/Lucknow), and [Bengaluru](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bangalore-India) in the following days. The movement held a second protest in Delhi on June 20, supported by various left-leaning student organizations and later farmer unions. The organizers, led by Dipke, decided they would continue their protest indefinitely until their demands were met—overstaying beyond the period authorized by the Delhi Police. On June 28 Wangchuk began a [hunger strike](https://www.britannica.com/topic/hunger-strike) at the protest grounds demanding Pradhan’s resignation. He also sought speedy resolution of talks with the government around [constitutional](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/constitutional) demands in his home territory of [Ladakh](https://www.britannica.com/place/Ladakh). The fast lasted 20 days before he was forcibly removed from the protest grounds by police officials and taken to a hospital. Dipke called for Modi’s resignation thereafter and sat on hunger strike himself until being asked to call it off by Wangchuk. Organizers intensified the campaign by calling on protestors to march to the [Parliament of India](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Parliament-of-India) on July 20.

Protesters came in huge numbers for the CJP-led march to Parliament. Despite police barricades, crowd control, and restrictions on movement, supporters spilled into the streets and marched through central Delhi toward the Parliament, though they were impeded from reaching its [premises](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/premises) by the police, who used [tear gas](https://www.britannica.com/technology/tear-gas) and baton charging to push back crowds. In response to the [police action](https://www.britannica.com/topic/police-action) against protests, Wangchuk said he would continue his fast at the hospital. He ended his fast on July 24 after meeting a delegation of union ministers and public [assurances](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/assurances) of action on examination mismanagement by Modi, but the CJP said it would continue the protest until Pradhan tendered his resignation.

On July 21 members of the [Indian National Congress](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-National-Congress) and other opposition parties demonstrated in front of Modi’s official residence demanding accountability for the police’s crackdown against peaceful protestors. Several members, including leader of opposition [Rahul Gandhi](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Rahul-Gandhi), were detained and removed by the police.

In the days following the Parliament march, tensions were high in New Delhi as protestors continued to occupy the Jantar Mantar protest grounds and neighboring areas. Subway lines were closed off and several central paramilitary troops were flown in. On July 25 Pradhan tendered his resignation as education minister. Soon after this key demand was met, CJP spokespersons announced at a press conference that the group was ending the protest “in good faith” after the government assured it that its other demands—compensation to the families of the students who died by suicide and revocation of police cases filed against student protesters—would be met.

## Hurdles for the Cockroach Janta Party

The Cockroach Janta Party’s whirlwind rise on social media was not without setbacks. Its account on [X](https://www.britannica.com/money/Twitter) (formerly Twitter) was withheld within five days of its creation. Within a week of CJP’s online existence Dipke claimed the parody account and his personal handle on Instagram had been hacked. Moreover, he [alleged](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/alleged) that he and his family had received death threats online. Dipke also took pains to distance the movement from comparisons to violent Gen Z uprisings in [Nepal](https://www.britannica.com/place/Nepal) and [Bangladesh](https://www.britannica.com/place/Bangladesh), saying the CJP would maintain peaceful dissent within the bounds of the [Constitution of India](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Constitution-of-India). How much influence he held over CJP’s following on social media or otherwise was unclear, however. In its initial days, Dipke dissociated the movement from reported calls for protests on the ground in CJP’s name, which he viewed as potentially inflammatory. However, he later declared that CJP would lead a peaceful protest against unfair practices in the education system.
