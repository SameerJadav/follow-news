Cyclone Arnab's landfall process started along the Andhra Pradesh coast on Wednesday around 1:10am, bringing strong winds and heavy rainfall to several areas, the Indian Meteorological Department said.

In its bulletin issued at 1.10am on October 28, the IMD said Arnab’ was moving westwards over the west central Bay of Bengal, close to the Andhra Pradesh coast and Yanam, at a speed of 10 kmph.

IMD estimated the storm’s intensity at 125-135 kmph, with gusts reaching 150 kmph.

The storm is expected to cross the coast between Bapatla and Visakhapatnam, south of Yanam, between 2 am and 4 am. Winds are likely to gradually weaken after landfall.

Authorities remain on high alert as rescue and relief teams continue to monitor the situation and take precautionary measures to minimise the impact of the severe cyclonic storm.

Andhra Pradesh and Odisha had stepped up preparations on Tuesday ahead of the expected landfall of very severe cyclonic storm Arnab, with thousands of people moved to safety, nearly 4,000 cyclone shelters readied and at least 50 trains cancelled across the region.

Air travel was also affected, with at least 21 flights cancelled at Vijayawada and Visakhapatnam airports on Tuesday. The cancellations affected services to Chennai, Bengaluru, Visakhapatnam and Kadapa. An official said 15 flights scheduled to operate from Vijayawada airport on Wednesday had also been cancelled.

The Andhra Pradesh government had arranged 2,131 cyclone shelters across 20 districts and placed 1,087 medical teams on standby in areas likely to be affected. Officials said 8,281 emergency beds and 1,274 ambulances had also been made available.

Authorities had moved people out of vulnerable areas and shifted 1,351 pregnant women to hospitals. The government had stocked two months' worth of essential commodities at 14,686 ration shops, while cyclone alerts had been sent to the mobile phones of 2.70 crore people.

The South Coast Railway had cancelled 24 trains scheduled for Wednesday, while the East Coast Railway had cancelled 22. The South Eastern Railway had also cancelled four trains, officials said.

IMD director general Mrunjay Mohapatra had warned of potentially damaging winds along the coast.

Odisha had intensified its preparations, with heavy rain already reported in parts of the state. Schools had been closed in 16 of its 30 districts, while more than 30,000 people had been moved to cyclone shelters against a target of 48,000, a revenue department official said. Entry to sea beaches had been prohibited.

Special Relief Commissioner (SRC) Rajesh P Patil said 135 permanent cyclone shelters and around 1,600 temporary shelters had been prepared to accommodate people from vulnerable areas.

In neighbouring Jharkhand, the Ranchi district administration had ordered schools to remain closed on October 28 and 29 as authorities prepared for adverse weather.

A yellow alert for heavy rain had been issued for Ranchi, Khunti, Simdega, Gumla, Lohardaga, Saraikela-Kharsawan, East Singhbhum and West Singhbhum for 24 hours from 8.30 am on October 28.

The names of more than 99.3 lakh voters have been deleted from Karnataka’s final voter list published on Tuesday following the special intensive revision of electoral rolls, the Hindustan Times reported.

The number of voters fell to 4.5 crore from 5.5 crore that were on the state’s electoral rolls before the exercise began. This amounts to a decrease of about 17.9%.

In the final stage of the exercise, more than 8.6 lakh names were reinstated to the draft list, which had excluded over one crore voters in August.

Persons who applied for inclusion through Form 6 after Friday, when the period for claims and objections ended, would be added through supplementary lists to be published on a quarterly basis, The Hindu reported. Form 6 is used to register new voters.

Also read: Karnataka poll officials sought deletions of 61,000 voters without citing reasons: ‘The Hindu’

Karnataka is among the 19 states and Union Territories where the voter roll revision is being carried out in the third phase of SIR.

The enumeration phase of the exercise in Karnataka began on June 30.

At the end of the draft stage, 65.4 lakh voters had been marked as permanently shifted, 12.3 lakh as dead, 15.1 lakh as absent and 7 lakh as duplicate entries. The highest deletions were recorded in Bengaluru’s urban districts.

The state Election Commission had said at the time that notices would be sent to 43 lakh persons whose enumeration forms either had anomalies or those who had not been mapped.

In 2025, the first phase of the exercise was conducted in Bihar. It was extended to 12 states and Union Territories in the second phase in late 2025 and the first half of 2026.

Since the exercise began, concerns have been raised that the revision could eliminate genuine voters from the rolls and even shape electoral outcomes, as Scroll has reported.

In May, the Supreme Court upheld the legality of the revision. But it said that the exercise does not mean that the Election Commission can decide on whether the person is an Indian citizen.

Despite this, Bharatiya Janata Party-ruled states have cited the SIR to deny ration cards to those who have been eliminated from the rolls. In some cases, the police have denied permission for their passports to be renewed.

Since October 2, nationwide protests have been held calling for Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar’s resignation over concerns about the functioning of the Election Commission and the alleged irregularities in how the voter roll is being revised.

The Supreme Court on Tuesday criticised the Centre for failing to adequately justify steep markups on medicines, asking why it took years to cap trade margins on selected anti-cancer drugs and whether commercial considerations were being prioritised over patients’ lives.

A bench of Justices Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta reserved its order in a public interest litigation (PIL) filed in 2023 by advocate and activist Kishan Chand Jain, seeking affordable medicines and reforms in drug pricing and medical regulation.

The court also questioned hospitals’ pricing practices, asking why medicines were sold to patients at maximum retail prices (MRPs) that were far above their procurement costs.

“The lives of people, Ms Bhati… don’t take it as commerce. Treat it as the life of the poorest citizen of this country,” the bench told additional solicitor general Aishwarya Bhati, appearing for the Centre. “We have asked you no less than 30 times to tell us this justification,” it added.

The hearing took place after the government capped trade margins at 30% of the MRP for selected non-scheduled anti-cancer medicines through a notification issued on 17 October. According to the Centre’s submission, the measure is expected to reduce retail prices by 20-70%.

The bench also pulled up the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) over its delayed intervention and questioned whether the latest measure would meaningfully lower prices when the original MRP was itself substantially inflated. “Your body seems to be totally defunct, with due respect,” the bench remarked while questioning the authority’s record on addressing excessive markups.

The court cited the case of a chemotherapy injection that a medical dealer said he could procure for ₹11,000 but for which a hospital charged ₹1.3 lakh. It asked the government to explain the disparity and why it had taken so long to intervene.

The final ruling will determine the extent to which the Supreme Court can intervene to address excessive medicine mark-ups and whether broader price regulation is warranted. The decision will be closely watched by the pharmaceutical industry, hospitals and policymakers, given its potential implications for the Centre’s pricing policy and patients’ out-of-pocket healthcare costs.

Bhati argued that the government’s policy sought to balance the availability of essential medicines at reasonable prices with innovation, competition, industry growth and employment. It favoured targeted interventions over comprehensive price controls, she submitted, cautioning that excessive regulation could affect investment and the availability of medicines.

“Our policy is based on these four limbs,” she told the court, referring to the availability of essential medicines at reasonable prices, innovation, competition and industry growth, including employment and shared economic well-being.

The bench, however, questioned the adequacy of the existing framework, asking why a medicine’s initial MRP could be set far above its supply price and then be allowed to rise annually. “You at the initial level fix the MRP right up to the moon. Then you increase by 10%,” the bench remarked.

It also challenged the government’s reliance on market forces to regulate prices, saying patients had limited bargaining power and little information about the prices at which hospitals and retailers procured medicines.

Senior advocate Mukul Rohatgi, appearing for hospitals, argued that medicine margins helped private healthcare facilities meet the costs of maintaining operating theatres, catheterisation laboratories, MRI machines and round-the-clock medical staff. He said public hospitals were overstretched and private facilities needed adequate resources to provide healthcare.

Rohatgi warned that restricting medicine margins could force hospitals to raise charges for other services. He also said listed hospitals typically operated on profit margins of around 10% and needed to invest thousands of crores of rupees in upgrading equipment. “These hospitals are listed companies… 10% is the profit of all these hospitals. I have to bring in thousands of crores every year to update machinery,” he told the bench.

The bench suggested that medicine packaging display both the MRP and the price to retailer (PTR) to improve transparency. It also raised concerns about whether financial incentives arising from high medicine margins could encourage unnecessary procedures, though it clarified it was not making a definitive finding that such practices had occurred.

The top court referred to a recent case where a medical distributor's wife was admitted to a hospital for cancer treatment.
