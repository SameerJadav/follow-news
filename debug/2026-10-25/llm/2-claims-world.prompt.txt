Nearly 1,000 arrests were made as hundreds of thousands of people took part in another day of protests across France which were followed by some clashes late on Saturday, officials have said.

After a day of largely peaceful demonstrations, police fired tear gas and water cannon to disperse protesters in a number of cities including Paris, while barricades were set alight in Marseille and people ripped boards from a shop in Bordeaux.

But officials have so far managed to avoid rioting and looting on the scale seen last weekend, which resulted in some of the worst scenes of disorder in France's modern history.

The interior ministry said an estimated 410,000 people protested on Saturday, while the unions put the number at about 800,000.

Katya Adler: France divided over who to blame for riots and what to learn from them

Some 70,000 police officers were deployed and restrictions imposed in several cities to prevent the latest protests over the cost of living and poor school conditions from descending into the kind of chaos which saw officers outnumbered, hundreds of businesses looted and buildings torched.

President Emmanuel Macron had decreed earlier this week that only demonstrations organised by a recognised trade unions would be permitted. Hundreds of people have been jailed for vandalism, arson and looting after fast-track court hearings in recent days.

Clashes between protesters and riot police in Paris erupted at the end of a peaceful march from the city's Porte d'Italie towards Porte de Vincennes on Saturday, where the police operation included an elite unit that usually deals with organised crime and counter-terrorism.

Some protesters were seen erecting makeshift barricades and pelting officers with projectiles, while police baton-charged groups of masked youths who were smashing the windows of a car dealership.

French Interior Minister Laurent NuÃ±ez said that "several thousand radical activists gathered at the rear of the march, including around 500 individuals identified as rioters", the AFP news agency reported.

In the southern port of Marseille, rubbish bins were set ablaze and metal sheets used to block streets. A law enforcement vehicle was seen driving through a burning barricade in France's second-largest city.

Meanwhile, some protesters in dark clothing and face coverings were seen taking boards off a building to build a street barricade in the southern city of Bordeaux.

In Lyon, police used tear gas grenades to disperse a crowd which had reportedly gathered in an area deemed off-limits by the local prefecture.

"There were around 150 of us, mostly secondary school and university students. Then the tear gas came almost immediately. You could barely see anything in the street," said one protester in France's third-largest city.

NuÃ±ez said late in the day that nearly 1,000 people had been arrested, a number he said would increase.

The minister also said two people had been "seriously injured": one in the south-western city of Toulouse, and another one in Clermont-Ferrand, central France.

Earlier, a peaceful protester in Paris who gave her name as Zelie told the BBC people had taken to the streets because they were "really frustrated" and had been "suffering a lot" since Macron came to office.

"They keep us quiet. They take our money, they tax us a lot and we can't say anything. Today we are in the streets because it's been too long, it's been too much," the 26-year-old said.

Two masked teenage protesters, who did not want to give their names, also said their generation was in despair and felt abandoned by the government.

"I think looting is really not good," one of the demonstrators said of last Saturday's disorder. "But it's because of poverty and the violence of the police."

He added that the protests would end "when we have a response to our demands. We really want a change in France".

Meanwhile, some 13,000 people joined a rally against the hard-right National Rally party in the north-central city of OrlÃ©ans which proceeded peacefully.

Mass protests triggered by similar issues as in France also rocked several other European cities on Saturday.

In Albania's capital Tirana, riot police fired tear gas to disperse thousands of anti-government demonstrators, while clashes were also reported in Belgium's capital Brussels.

In Milan, northern Italy, a major security operation including hundreds of soldiers alongside police prevented protesters gathering in the city. Many had feared a repeat of violent scenes last week when some started fires, smashed windows and looted shops.

Around 550,000 people took part in last weekend's demonstrations against rising fuel prices and the cost of living, with frustrations escalating after weeks of student protests. The chaos which followed resulted in more than 2,500 arrests.

ROME: Security forces deployed massively in Italy on Saturday (Oct 24) for protests in which thousands rallied despite a sweeping crackdown in the wake of violent clashes last weekend.

Police said they arrested nine people and briefly detained 371 more, including 110 minors, who were taken to police stations in eight cities as clashes broke out at some protests.

Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's hard-right government approved tough security measures this week and police had carried out dozens of searches and arrests in advance of the rallies.

"Marching is a right. We need to make our voices heard," Alessio Scimone, a 24-year-old betting shop worker, one of the protesters in Milan, told AFP.

Several thousand people marched through Milan, bringing traffic in the centre to a standstill.

A similar number rallied in Rome, where some protesters briefly broke into the Colosseum.

Police "managed to isolate and contain the violent fringes, with balance and professionalism," Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi was quoted by Italian news agency ANSA as saying.

"In Italy there can be no room for those who delude themselves that they can act with impunity," he said.

In Milan, some stores closed early and others boarded their windows following outbreaks of looting in the fashion hub last weekend.

Others remained defiantly open after a call by the city's mayor Giuseppe Sala not to "panic".

"We want to give the message that we are not scared by these kids," said Alessandro Buhne, 30, who works at the Philipp Plein menswear shop.

"Tourists are scared of this situation so we want to give the signal that you are safe if you enter our store," he said.

Police on Saturday also said they had arrested a 30-year-old man suspected of being the figure behind the "October 17 Movement", a group which had called for the protests.

The demonstrations are part of a wave of protests by students, truck drivers and farmers over cost of living issues like housing and fuel prices.

The government this week approved financial measures to help businesses dealing with the spike in fuel prices seen after the Middle East war.

The truck blockades that had paralysed southern Italy, leading to acute fuel and food shortages for a week, have now been lifted.

But hours later, SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, whose company owns Starlink, accused the Ukrainian military of violating the company's terms. Neither Musk nor the US government, which operates Starshield using Starlink technology, had taken responsibility for imposing the curbs.

Both Musk and US officials said publicly that any decisions on Starlink and Starshield limitations could only have been taken by the other.

Reuters has reported that the order to throttle Starshield came from inside the White House. Previously, US administrations had several times asked SpaceX to turn Starlink on or off in various countries, sources said.

On Friday, contradicting his previous statement that the services had been fully restored, Trump said that if problems persisted, he would "get it straightened out".

Israeli troops have levelled a neighbourhood in Hebron, driving out hundreds of families in collective punishment for a militant attack on an army checkpoint outside the West Bank city, local Palestinian authorities said.

The whole city of 250,000 people was sealed off by checkpoints on every point of entry.

One video showed soldiers marking Palestinian houses with a sprayed black cross, another showed a bulldozer tearing down a stone wall on a hillside and another showed a large residential building festooned with Israeli flags.

The large-scale demolitions follow an attack on Friday on an Israeli military checkpoint outside Hebron by two gunmen. After a 20-minute gunfight, two Israeli soldiers were killed, three injured and the two gunmen were also killed.

On October 25, Brazilians will return to the polls for the second round of the presidential election. The results will matter not only for the future of country itself, but also for the region and the world.

Alongside Canada and Mexico, Brazil, under the leadership of current President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, is one of the few countries in the region that has stood up to Donald Trump’s meddling and interference attempts.

Under Lula, Brazil still serves as a bulwark against the imposition of right-wing populist agendas critics say run counter to the broader interests of local societies, but cater to the interests of certain domestic elites. It is within this context that we must consider the Brazilian election alongside other electoral processes in the democratic world.

Many Trump-style leaders have recently come to power in the continent. The Economist compiled descriptive statistics to try to capture the trend. According to the publication, in 2023 there were two or three presidents aligned with Trump in the region. Today there are 11. If elected, Flávio Bolsonaro – son of jailed former president Jair Bolsonaro – would be the 12th. Should that happen, the alignment of South and Central America with the current US administration would be further advanced.

One theoretical school of thought in international relations that attempts to explain this phenomenon is “peripheral realism”. According to this theory, by being realistic in their assessment that they possess few resources and limited economic, military, institutional or diplomatic capacity, countries assume a subordinate position. In other words, they become merely a wagon on the US locomotive.

There are people advocating along these lines today in Colombia and Argentina. And the prevailing thinking within Flávio Bolsonaro’s inner circle also appears to align with peripheral realism. Publicly sympathetic to Donald Trump, the candidate himself has already stated at rallies that, if elected, he will join Trump’s regional security initiative, the Shield of the Americas, “to fight narco-terrorism.”

Some articles and campaign materials from Flávio Bolsonaro’s campaign already make this clear. If there is a changing of the guard – with the challenger defeating the incumbent in Brazil – the country is likely to join this group of nations aligned with the White House that includes Argentina and Colombia – respectively the second and third largest countries in South America, after Brazil by far.

Peripheral realism is, therefore, the idea of accepting one’s peripheral status and acting in the most pragmatic way, normalizing submission to the interests of the most powerful country. In doing so, one relinquishes the concept of sovereign equality among peoples – a pillar of international law – in order to acknowledge the asymmetry between nations and, from there, attempt to extract some advantage.

The problem is that these advantages are, almost invariably, directed toward the ruling elite of the submissive country. Society, in general, ends up having to bear the cost of this foreign policy choice. And this is not a theory that is being made up now; it already has a history. In the 1990s, for example, the famous Argentine theorist, Carlos Escudé – a mentor to then Argentine President Carlos Menem – advocated for this school of thought.

There are other examples. Costa Rica, for example, has no army having relinquished it and handed over the security function to the United States. It is a classic example of how a central tenet of sovereignty – having an armed force to protect the territory and the population – can be sacrificed under peripheral realism.

In the event of a victory for Flávio Bolsonaro on October 25, I believe one of the first steps would be for Brazil to join Trump’s Shield of the Americas, an alliance formed under the pretext of fighting organised crime in the Americas. This could pave the way for more US foreign intervention in areas related to international security.
