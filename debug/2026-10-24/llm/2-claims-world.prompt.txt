Jean-Noël Barrot, the French foreign minister, said another war between Iran and the US would be bad for both sides, and only worsen rising oil prices, which have hit their highest level since May. Ed Miliband, the UK foreign secretary, also urged both sides to embrace diplomacy.

The Istanbul meeting, an unprecedented gathering of regional and European leaders initiated by Turkey and Germany, represented a rare chance for European ministers to have any input into the crises afflicting the Middle East, including in Yemen, Palestine and Iran. They have largely been left as bystanders, apart from sending air force protection to Saudi Arabia as it tries to fend off Houthi drone and missile assaults on its airports and energy installations.

The Middle Eastern states were represented by Fidan, Egypt’s Badr Abdelatty, Pakistan’s Ishaq Dar and Saudi Arabia’s Faisal bin Farhan. Pakistan, Turkey and Saudi Arabia formed the Mecca defensive security pact in August, a grouping Egypt may join.

The European side was represented by Barrot, Miliband, Germany’s Johann Wadephul and Italy’s Antonio Tajani. The agenda covered the Palestinian issue, including settlement expansion, settler violence and the two-state solution, as well as security in the strait of Hormuz, Yemen and efforts to end the Russia-Ukraine war and prevent spillover into the Black Sea.

Wadephul said: “We are united by the impact these deadlocked conflicts are having on the economy and security of our countries and by the will to seek new ways to bring about solutions.”

He made no criticism of the US but accused “the Houthis and Iran of blackmailing world trade and continuing to stranglehold global supplies of energy and fertiliser”.

“We are feeling the effects of the attacks on shipping routes and whether at the petrol pump or the supermarket,” he said. “Elsewhere there is threat of famine.”

Tajani said the aim of the meeting was to work for “de-escalation to guarantee freedom of navigation”.

“I do not believe the problem can be solved from a purely military standpoint,” he added. “If we want to reopen Hormuz, which is vital for our economies, we must promote dialogue and make Iran understand that this attitude cannot continue.”

NEW YORK: The headlines were unremarkable. Researchers had found a way to weaponise two vulnerabilities in PHP, a programming language used for websites and apps, and companies were being urged on Wednesday (Oct 21) to install a patch.

A smattering of websites and services were taken offline while it was installed. The US said it was looking into government systems and expediting mitigations. The media barely blinked.

But like a thwarted terror attack, or the Year 2000 problem, the general feeling of a nothingburger can disguise the behind-the-scenes heroics that have - so far - averted catastrophe.

That’s the case here: an incident that should be considered the most significant victory for the good guys so far in the new AI-cybersecurity age, a boon for the cybersecurity industry eager to prove it’s more valuable than ever.

What nonsense that seems today, with the opposite proving true.

The new paradigm of security threats has made cybersecurity expertise more valuable, so it’s no surprise that Cloudflare shares rose around 7 per cent on the news, up 76 per cent since that February shock. Crowdstrike is up 146 per cent in that time.

Of course, the AI firms are helping defend against a risk they helped create, but that cat’s out of the bag now - the same technology that can discover new drugs or write complex code can be used to hack computers. The only course of action is to deal with it.

This incident should provide encouragement. The takeaway is that a collaboration between security researchers and frontier AI labs gave the world a head start against a serious threat. These collaborative efforts must continue and be expanded.
