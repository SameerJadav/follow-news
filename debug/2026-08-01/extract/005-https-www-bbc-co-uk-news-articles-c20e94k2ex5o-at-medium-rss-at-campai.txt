In addition to a 15-point roadmap published by the US-led Board of Peace, the BBC has seen a four-page plan for the phased disarmament of Hamas and gradual Israeli withdrawal.

Hamas giving up its weapons - as predicted - has been a main sticking point in advancing President Trump's 20-point plan for ending the war in Gaza.

That plan, unveiled in September last year, included the creation of the Board of Peace to oversee the agreement's implementation and "handle the funding for the redevelopment of Gaza".

The principles in the roadmap and the document seen by the BBC say: "A process shall begin to inventory and store heavy weapons, military production sites, weapons depots, and tunnels". It says this is conditional on Israel meeting its commitments under the initial ceasefire agreement reached in October last year.

The process would be overseen by the Palestinian National Committee - a group of independent technocrats to which Hamas has agreed to hand over power - and with the deployment of the International Stabilisation Force to Gaza. The document refers not just to Hamas but other Palestinian factions joining the disarmament process.

The roadmap documents then links this "to a phased Israeli withdrawal" and specifies that "no weapons shall be transferred or handed over to Israel or to any non-Palestinian party".

The exact mechanism of how the handover of weapons would work is not clear. It is believed that all heavy weapons â including Hamas's remaining rockets - and other military hardware would be moved into a secure storage managed by the National Committee. As yet, the committee has not entered Gaza. It has been working from Cairo.

In terms of timeframe, the document we have seen says only that this would happen "in a gradual, sequential, and time-bound manner, based on an implementation schedule to be prepared within 14 days."

Hamas has since said giving up its heavy weapons is contingent on Israel ending "all forms of aggression" and withdrawing its forces from Gaza.

Israel's commitment to ending its attack on Gaza is a "fundamental prerequisite" for moving forward with the deal, the group said in a statement.

Underground tunnel networks, weapons stores and manufacturing sites would be mapped and destroyed.

Remaining personal arms and police weapons would need to be registered and handed over in stages to the new Palestinian police force working under the supervision of the International Stabilisation Force (ISF). Hundreds of new Palestinian police recruits have already been trained in Egypt and Jordan. Israel's cabinet recently approved some 200 international troops from "friendly countries" entering Gaza as part of the ISF, but it has not yet been deployed.

Hamas has been seeking assurances that pro-Israel militia in Gaza will be dismantled and will also give up their arms.

Israel is deeply sceptical about Hamas' willingness to give up its weapons or its control in Gaza.

Israeli officials have insisted that there will be no Israeli withdrawal from the Yellow Line - which demarcates the area it controls - before Hamas is disarmed.

What makes this more unclear is that Israel currently controls more than the 53% of Gaza in which its troops were set to remain under the terms of the October ceasefire deal. As the BBC has recently reported, yellow concrete blocks delineating the Yellow Line have been moved westwards so that Israel controls more than 60% of the strip.

Part of the document seen by the BBC is an annex. This requires Israeli forces to withdraw to the agreed Yellow Line "within 24 hours of the Israeli government's approval of the plan." It says they should not return "as long as Hamas complies with the implementation of the agreement."

Hamas announced earlier this month that it had dissolved its government in Gaza and was preparing to transfer power to the National Committee as part of the ceasefire deal. In negotiations it has wanted to ensure that the tens of thousands of civil servants that its government employed in Gaza prior to the war should be compensated or given new jobs.

The public workers include Hamas police and security forces but also health and education staff. Many, though not all, are likely to be supporters of Hamas.

The plan agrees to treat all public employees "lawfully, fairly, and with dignity regarding their rights."
