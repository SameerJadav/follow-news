Donald Trump and Chinese president Xi Jinping wrapped up their Washington summit on Friday, facing criticism that they had squandered an opportunity to pause their countries’ artificial intelligence (AI) arms race.

The three-day state visit emphasised pageantry and personal rapport rather than substantial agreements. The US president and Xi discussed the wars in Iran and Ukraine and the status of Taiwan. Their biggest policy announcement was a modest two-month extension of a trade truce.

But despite the presence of top tech executives at a White House state dinner on Thursday night, Trump and Xi failed to deliver the AI breakthrough that some experts and foreign governments had hoped for amid warnings that the technology could pose an existential threat to humanity.

“It was such a layup,” said Ro Khanna, the top Democrat on the House of Representatives’ select committee on China. “If [Trump] had just said, look, we have established technical working groups and we’ve agreed to a framework that has called for DeepSeek and Moonshot and Alibaba to sit down with Anthropic and OpenAI and Gemini and with technical experts and they’re going to commit to at least a pause on any self-improving AI, he could have looked like he was a statesman taking the lead.

“I believe Xi Jinping would have agreed to some of at least the technical working groups. But he [Trump] didn’t want to; he has no interest in doing that.”

It was the first visit to the White House by a Chinese president in more than a decade and Xi’s second state visit to the US. Three days of pomp and circumstance culminated on Friday with Trump and Xi drinking tea. Trump said ⁠the summit ⁠had produced great strides and that “our ​farmers are going to be happy,” but did not provide details.

The pair then went to the National Archives, where Trump and Xi looked at the original Declaration of Independence, the constitution and Bill of Rights. Trump told reporters they were comparing China’s documents to America’s founding documents. “Ours goes 250 years, which is great, and we’re proud of. Theirs goes 6,000 years. So there’s a little difference between 6,000 and 250. But we’re very proud of our 250.”

On Thursday, Xi acknowledged that the US and China have the “responsibility to develop and manage AI for good” and “ensure that the development of AI is always ​under human control”. China aims to have next-generation smart terminals and AI agents reach more than 90% penetration by 2030.

But Trump – who has repeatedly said the US must forge ahead to maintain its lead over China – appeared more focused on his push to rebrand artificial intelligence as “super intelligence”, later claiming on social media that Xi “seems to like” the term without divulging the substance of their discussions.

Later Trump hosted a black-tie state dinner attended by titans of the tech industry. Bernie Sanders, an independent senator of Vermont, used social media to label a photo of Trump and Xi’s table with guests’ net worth: Elon Musk of SpaceX and Tesla ($928bn), Lisa Su of Advanced Micro Devices ($3.5bn), Jensen Huang of Nvidia ($186bn) and Tim Cook of Apple ($3.1bn). Sanders commented: “A government of, by and for the billionaire class.”

But their counterparts in the Chinese tech industry were not present and there was little sign of either country making even a symbolic gesture to apply the brakes.

Khanna commented: “Trump didn’t have to have some groundbreaking treaty like Reagan and Gorbachev did [on nuclear weapons]. Jonathan Czin, a fellow in the China Center at the Brookings Institution thinktank in Washington, agrees that an opportunity was missed. “We are in this potentially transformative moment for both countries and for the world and for technology and, instead of having a meeting that would meet the moment, you got theatre of the absurd in lieu of that,” he said.

“Even going into this meeting, you’re dealing with a septuagenarian and octogenarian who may or may not have ever had first-hand contact with actually using AI and what happens with this technology rests on their two shoulders. When US President Donald Trump rolled out the red carpet for Chinese leader Xi Jinping at Andrews Air Force Base on Wednesday evening, it was a rare diplomatic gesture that has only happened a handful of times in US history.

Generally, world leaders are received by the president at the White House. Xi's VIP reception underlines the importance Trump places on his personal rapport with China's most powerful leader in a generation.

After 90 minutes of bilateral talks in the Oval Office, Trump spent Thursday afternoon showing his "good friend" Xi his various White House construction projects, including the helipad completed just days earlier over the South Lawn. Trump then told reporters "we had a great meeting," adding that Xi "loves good granite."

The optics continued at a glitzy state dinner Thursday night, where Trump said he and President Xi have "never gotten along better" and lauded building a "relationship that promotes prosperity and security for future generations."

Judging from Xi's mannerisms, it isn't clear the Chinese leader shares Trump's enthusiasm for personal diplomacy. When Trump arrived in Beijing for a summit in May, Xi sent diplomatic envoy, Vice President Han Zheng, to meet the US president.

Xi's whirlwind visit to the US, his first in 10 years, has been heavy on pomp but relatively low on substance.

As of Friday morning, most summit coverage has focused on what was for dinner and the tech CEO guest list, in lieu of any deliverables on thorny issues like trade deals or cooperation on artificial intelligence. There is still time for announcements when Xi departs Washington later Friday after visiting the National Archives with Trump.

"The summit did signal a temporary detente, even if concrete deliverables on issues such as AI regulation and trade tensions remained limited. For either government to take steps that would rupture the truce, approval would likely be required at the highest political level, namely from the two presidents," Dominic Chiu, senior analyst for China and Northeast Asia at the political risk consultancy Eurasia Group, told DW.

"The summit's optics therefore matter. By meeting publicly and emphasizing stability, both leaders have raised the political cost of actions that could rapidly destabilize the relationship," he added.

The most-pressing issue remains managing a trade war launched by Trump's scattershot tariffs in April 2025 that at one point were so high they essentially amounted to an embargo on Chinese goods.

On Wednesday, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said a trade truce due to expire in November would be extended until January "to give us more time to see what we can do on the economic front." Such an extension had been widely expected before the summit.

The trade truce builds on the "Busan deal" Trump and Xi agreed on at a meeting in South Korea last October, where Trump backed off on more tariffs in exchange for Xi easing export control for rare earths, which are critical ingredients for building high-tech products like smartphones.

Leveraging China's chokehold on rare earths to confront Trump's tariff blitz represented a major victory for Xi.

The Financial Times reported earlier this week that the US "believes China has not fully lived up to its commitment of uninterrupted supply," adding that China's shipments of rare earths to the US have dropped recently, amid concerns China could again "choke supplies."

The French Catholic hierarchy strongly opposed the measures, in keeping with Catholic teaching that human life must be protected and defended from conception until natural death.

In his speech to Macron and other government and diplomatic authorities, Leo reminded France of its "deep Christian roots" and called especially for the French to remember that the right to life is the "indispensable foundation of every other human right."

The US Supreme Court on Friday allowed the Trump administration to resume using an expanded federal database to help states verify the citizenship status of registered voters, setting aside a lower court order that had blocked the system.

The justices granted the Justice Department’s request for a stay of the ruling by a federal judge in Washington, DC. The decision allows the administration to continue using the modified Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements, or SAVE, programme while the legal challenge proceeds.

The database, which has traditionally been used by government agencies to verify immigration and citizenship status for benefits, was expanded by the administration in 2025.

The revised system allows state and local authorities to conduct bulk searches and use Social Security numbers and other information held by the Social Security Administration.

Under the system, election officials can submit information about voters to SAVE. If the database cannot confirm a person’s citizenship, officials can ask the individual to provide additional proof of citizenship.

The administration has argued that the system is intended to help states identify noncitizens who are registered to vote. They argued that the revised system could rely on outdated information and potentially affect naturalised US citizens whose records have not been updated.

In its dissent, Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor and Elena Kagan, said the court was allowing the system to continue despite concerns about harm to lawful voters.

Jackson pointed to evidence that some naturalised citizens could be required to provide additional proof of citizenship because federal records did not accurately reflect their status.

A federal district judge had previously found that the modified SAVE programme violated federal law and vacated the changes. The US Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit subsequently declined to stay that ruling, prompting the administration to seek emergency relief from the Supreme Court.

Friday’s order does not finally resolve the underlying legal dispute. The Supreme Court has stayed the lower court’s June order while the appeal continues and potentially until the justices consider a further petition for review.

The decision means the expanded SAVE system can again be used for voter-citizenship verification, even as its legality and the accuracy of the information it provides remain subjects of litigation.

Internet and call services have been disrupted in Ethiopia's northern region of Tigray following the outbreak of fighting this week between regional forces and the national army.

Services provided by state-owned telecoms operator Ethio Telecom have been completely cut, although those from the smaller private firm Safaricom remain operational. Internet monitoring group NetBlocks earlier posted on X that network data indicated a sub-national internet shutdown in northern Tigray.

"Last night telecommunications got knocked out," Duke Burbridge, an American humanitarian worker in Tigray's capital, Mekelle, told the BBC's Newsday programme.

Sources have told the Reuters news agency that the group which controls Tigray, the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF), ordered the communications blackout, with one suggesting it was to disrupt government drones.

The BBC is unable to report from Tigray and surrounding areas.

Even before the current disruption, finding video and images to establish what is happening on the ground has been a challenge.

So far, other than footage from Mekelle, most of what has been shared online has been from outside the region.

The young women who fought in Ethiopia's last civil war and don't want to see another one

Burbridge said there had been a scarcity of cash in Tigray for the past eight months, with people unable to access ATMs so the lack of internet services would be "devastating for everybody".

Many people rely on banking and mobile money applications for transactions, with the majority using Ethio Telecom and Safaricom's networks to access these services.

On Thursday a Mekelle resident who we are only identifying as Fitsum made a similar point.

With the outbreak of war, people were limiting their movements and the city was all quiet - "it's like a curfew has been placed," he said.

Clashes between Tigrayan forces and their allies and Ethiopia's federal troops broke out on Wednesday, prompting fears of a return to a full scale civil war and reports of panic buying.

A humanitarian source in the neighbouring region of Afar told the AFP news agency there was "heavy fighting overnight", including drone strikes, around the town of Abala in the mountains between Afar and Tigray, as "federal forces pushed the rebels from entering the town".

There have also been reports of fighting near the town of Lalibela in the Amhara region, which is home to churches hewn out of rock, recognised as a UN World Heritage site. The US has warned against travel to all three regions.

During the two-year conflict which ended in 2022, Tigray was totally cut off from the outside world.

Limited access to cash, food and medicine pushed hundreds of thousands into famine-like conditions.

It was one of the most deadly conflicts this century - with estimates that some 600,000 people died as the fighting drove the region to the precipice of famine.

On Sunday, the TPLF announced an alliance with six other armed groups to oust the government.

On Wednesday, its forces seized control of three airports from federal police.

Following the seizure of the airports, Ethiopian Airlines has cancelled all flights to Tigray.

[a2] DW World <https://www.dw.com/en/why-eritrea-matters-as-fighting-returns-to-ethiopia-s-tigray/a-79426582?maca=en-rss-en-world-4025-rdf> (FULL TEXT)
On September 23, fighting escalated dramatically in Ethiopia's Tigray region. Tigrayan forces seized three local airports and the conflict spread into the neighboring regions of Afar and Amhara, according to reports.

Both sides have accused the other of starting the escalation. The renewed fighting poses a major challenge to the 2022 Pretoria peace agreement that ended the previous Tigray war.

This is where Eritrea becomes crucial. Martin Plaut, a British journalist specializing in the Horn of Africa, noted that the US needs Eritrea in the struggle to secure the Red Sea against the Houthis, an Iran-backed Shiite Muslim militia.

Plaut argues that removing sanctions gives new political weight to one of Africa's most repressive regimes.

As fighting resumes in Tigray, Eritrea could once again become a decisive military player. Plaut said the current tensions had the potential of developing into a broader regional confrontation, which, he said, "is the most serious danger."

In his view, the administration of US President Donald Trump has already made a strategic calculation. "Trump has now mended fences with Eritrea and has lifted sanctions against Eritrea" in an effort to reduce the threat posed by the Houthis and secure the Red Sea, said Plaut.

The escalation in Tigray came only days after Washington removed key Eritrean entities and individuals from its sanctions list.

On September 18, the United States allowed the national emergency underpinning its Ethiopia-related sanctions regime to expire. The Treasury Department subsequently removed a number of Eritrean individuals and entities from its sanctions list, including senior financial official Hagos Ghebrehiwet and the Red Sea Trading Corporation. The measures had originally been imposed in 2021 in connection with the Tigray war.

US officials have not described the move as an endorsement of Eritrea, nor has Washington publicly said that it struck a deal with Eritrean President Isaias Afwerki. But it removes a layer of pressure on a government whose strategic importance has grown as security around the Red Sea deteriorates.

For Abdurahman Sayed, director of the Horn of Africa Centre for Regional Integration and a political analyst, the wider geopolitical context is crucial.

"It is happening at a time when the war on Iran is ongoing," he told DW. "The Strait of Hormuz has been blocked for some time, and now the Bab el-Mandeb on the Red Sea, where Eritrea is located — this may give Eritrea some advantage to establish a meaningful relationship with the US."

Eritrea controls a long coastline opposite Yemen. Its southern port of Assab lies close to the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, one of the world's most important maritime waterways.

Moses Okello, a senior researcher at the Institute for Security Studies in Addis Ababa, said the regional context has fundamentally changed since the previous Tigray war.

"In 2020, the concern was the TPLF [Tigray People's Liberation Front] versus the Ethiopian National Defense Force [ENDF]," he told DW. "Today, the entire Middle East is up in flames, and now the tensions in the Red Sea. So this changes the strategic value of not just the Eritrean coast, but the entire Red Sea waterway."

For Washington, cooperation — or at least non-hostility — from Eritrea can therefore be strategically valuable.

Eritrea is one of the world's most authoritarian states, with no national elections since independence in 1993 and an extensive system of indefinite national service. Eritrean forces also fought alongside Ethiopian federal troops during the 2020-22 Tigray war.

And the economic impact of lifting sanctions may be more limited than the political symbolism suggests. "Lifting the sanctions may not make a huge difference on the ground," said Sayed. "Eritrea is not a trade hub [nor does it have] a well-established trading relationship with the outside world. It's actually a closed country."

On September 21, seven Ethiopian armed and political groups announced an alliance aimed at removing Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government. The coalition includes the TPLF, the Amhara Fano National Movement and the Oromo Liberation Army. Some members were on opposing sides during the 2020-22 Tigray war.

It remains unclear how closely the alliance's members will coordinate militarily. The alliance brings together groups with very different histories that share opposition to the federal government.

Two days later, fighting escalated in Tigray. Tigrayan forces seized airports and fighting spread into neighboring regions. The TPLF has said federal forces attacked first; Addis Ababa has said the TPLF launched the offensive.

The renewed fighting has raised questions about the durability of the 2022 Pretoria agreement.

Fighting escalates after Tigrayan rebels form new coalition aimed at overthrowing Abiy Ahmed government

There are growing fears of a return to civil war in Ethiopia after an offensive by fighters from Tigray into the neighbouring states of Afar and Amhara and the formation of a new rebel alliance aimed at overthrowing the government of the prime minister, Abiy Ahmed.

About 600,000 people died between 2020 and 2022 during a devastating war between the Ethiopian army and the political party and paramilitary group the Tigray People’s Liberation Front (TPLF). Both sides are now accusing the other of triggering a return to open conflict.

On Sunday, seven armed groups from around the country, including the TPLF, announced the formation of the Ethiopian Peoples’ Forces Alliance for Survival, aimed at deposing the government in Addis Ababa. Then, on Wednesday, the TPLF seized control of three airports from federal police. The state-owned carrier, Ethiopian Airlines, said it had suspended flights to the region “due to the current situation”.

The TPLF also launched attacks into Afar and Amhara. A humanitarian source in Afar told Agence France-Presse there were heavy artillery attacks by the group on Thursday as they attempted to take control of the town of Yallo, near the Tigrayan border.

Flights to the historic Ethiopian town of Lalibela were also suspended on Thursday because of nearby fighting, while people in Tigray stocked up ⁠on food and cash, with one resident telling Reuters that people there feared they could face siege conditions similar to those during the previous war.

A resident of Adigrat, a city close to the Eritrean border, ‌described panic-buying and shops emptying. “Food shortages are worsening as market shelves sit empty … Essential medicines remain scarce and astronomically expensive,” he told Reuters. “Daily survival has become a constant calculation.”

The TPLF’s vice-president, Amanuel Assefa, told AFP: “We are in the situation of full-blown war now.” He confirmed Tigrayan forces were fighting alongside local armed groups in Afar and Amhara.

The group took control of Abala, also on the Tigray-Afar border, on Wednesday, a foreign security source based in Ethiopia told AFP, while a TPLF source said they had seized several other towns in Afar. The foreign security source also said there was fighting around the Amhara town of Weldiya – one of the few routes to the capital.

In its first public comments on the fighting, Ethiopia’s army said on ⁠Thursday it had foiled the attacks and killed, wounded or captured more than 500 enemy fighters.

Analysts say the TPLF has support from Ethiopia’s​ neighbour and historic adversary Eritrea, which fears landlocked Ethiopia is planning to invade its territory to gain access to the Red Sea. The TPLF’s Assefa told AFP “we have no information about Eritrea’s plan”. Asked if they would request Eritrean intervention, he said: “Not yet.”

The current fighting reflects shifting dynamics from the civil war, when Eritrea and Fano, an insurgent group that is now part of the new alliance, both fought with the government against the Tigrayans.

Speaking at the UN general assembly on Wednesday, the Ethiopian president, Taye Atske-Selassie Amde, decried “the forces of destruction and mayhem” and “interference in our internal affairs by adversarial elements”. He said Ethiopia had exercised “immense restraint” and made progress with its neighbours.

Abiy’s spokesperson, Billene Seyoum, said members of the alliance had been collaborating for a while ​with the financial backing of Eritrea. Eritrea ​has repeatedly denied similar accusations.

The TPLF ruled Ethiopia from 1991 to 2018 before being sidelined by the rise of Abiy, eventually triggering the civil war.

The renewed fighting marks the most serious challenge ​yet to the 2022 Pretoria agreement, which ended the civil war and maintained an uneasy calm until late last year, when clashes resumed.

It raises fears of a broader regional conflict that could draw in armed groups from across and further destabilise a region already strained by war in neighbouring Sudan and tensions along the Red Sea.

On Wednesday, the African Union chair, Mahmoud Ali Youssouf, urged “utmost restraint” from the warring parties and resumption of direct talks. An EU spokesperson called for “immediate de-escalation” and a recommitment to the Pretoria agreement.

In the 2020-22 war, Tigray was largely cut off from the outside world as communications were severed, and access to food, cash and medicines was severely restricted, pushing hundreds of thousands of people into famine-like conditions.

Fighting has erupted again in Ethiopia's northern Tigray region. Tigrayan forces launched a major offensive on September 23, with fighting also reported in the neighboring regions of Afar and Amhara.

The escalation threatens the fragile peace established by the 2022 Pretoria peace agreement, which ended the previous Tigray war between Ethiopia's federal government in Addis Ababa and the Tigray People's Liberation Front (TPLF).

But the central question is bigger than the return of war to Tigray: could the conflict connect with the wars and rivalries already destabilizing the Horn of Africa and the Red Sea?

Martin Plaut, a British journalist and Horn of Africa expert, argues the conflict could spread beyond Ethiopia's borders.

"If a conflict were to spill over, it is quite possible that a conflict involving Eritrea and Ethiopia could then involve Sudanese forces as well, which could drag the whole region into a war," he told DW in an exclusive interview.

Jalale Getachew Birru, a senior analyst for East Africa at the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED), also stressed that the conflict cannot be viewed in isolation. ACLED is an independent conflict-monitoring and research organization that collects and analyzes data on political violence and armed conflict around the world.

“The Ethiopia[n] case is not [an] isolated case for the Horn of Africa. It involves the neighboring countries as well, and it's being displayed across the Horn of Africa countries," Birru told DW.

Her assessment points to a broader regional dynamic: Ethiopia's conflict is unfolding alongside shifting alliances and competing interests in neighboring Sudan and Somalia. ACLED's current analysis likewise places the renewed Tigray conflict within a wider pattern of instability across the Horn of Africa.

The risk is not based on a single regional alliance. It comes from the fact that several conflicts are already geographically and politically connected, Birru noted.

The African Union (AU) has also expressed deep concern about the possibility of further destabilization.

In a statement on September 23, AU Commission Chairperson Mahmoud Ali Youssouf warned that new political and military alliances risk reversing the progress made since 2020.

The AU called for an immediate end to actions that could escalate tensions, renewed direct talks under the Pretoria agreement and restraint by Ethiopia's neighbors. It also tasked former Nigerian President Olusegun Obasanjo, the AU's high representative for the Horn of Africa, with supporting diplomatic efforts.

The AU's intervention reflects concerns that the crisis could have wider regional implications, including the possibility that it could interact with conflicts beyond Ethiopia's borders.

The TPLF recently joined six other Ethiopian armed groups in an alliance seeking to remove Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government. Among them is the Amhara Fano movement, which fought against the TPLF during the previous war.

The alliance is not a unified military force, and its members have different political objectives and territorial interests. But its formation could leave the Ethiopian government facing several fronts at once.

The fighting in Afar, a northeastern Ethiopian region bordering Eritrea and Djibouti, is particularly significant. The region contains the main transport corridor linking landlocked Ethiopia to Djibouti, through which much of Ethiopia's international trade passes. Sustained fighting there could therefore threaten a vital economic lifeline.

During the 2020–2022 Tigray war, Eritrean troops fought alongside Ethiopian federal forces against the TPLF. Since then, relations between Addis Ababa and Asmara, Eritrea's capital, have deteriorated. Ethiopia's ambitions for access to the Red Sea have added to Eritrea's security concerns.

"The war could certainly escalate, and Eritrea could become involved," Plaut said.

Ethiopia has accused Eritrea of supporting armed groups opposed to the government, while Asmara has denied such allegations. The extent of any direct Eritrean involvement remains difficult to establish independently.

Sudan has been at war since 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF). The conflict has drawn in regional powers and created one of the world's largest humanitarian crises.

Plaut argues that Ethiopia and Eritrea are already indirectly involved on opposing sides.

"The Ethiopians are on the side of the RSF and Hemedti," he said, referring to its commander Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo. "There has been strong evidence that there is a base in western Ethiopia which is supplying and training RSF fighters," Plaut added. DW has not independently verified that claim.
