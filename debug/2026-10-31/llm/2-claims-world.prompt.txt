=== STORY 0 [world] — Crypto exchange Binance targeted in record $6.3 billion cyber heist linked to North Korea ===

[a0] BBC World <https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cwe8lp7zy07zo?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss> (FULL TEXT)
Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, says it has frozen around half of the funds stolen in a "complex" hack, after $6.3bn (Â£4.7bn) was accessed in what is believed to be a record heist.

The firm said $3bn of its stolen digital assets had been secured - but many were still in the hands of unidentified hackers. It initially thought an engineer's laptop had been compromised, allowing unauthorised withdrawals, but later said the attack was wider.

Several analytics firms have linked the hack, which eclipses a $1.5bn (Â£1.12bn) heist on the Bybit exchange in 2025, to a North Korean hacking unit.

The US Secret Service said it was "working around the clock" "to identify, freeze, trace, and recover" stolen funds.

America's lead federal agency for investigating financial crimes added that it would "identify the cyber criminals responsible and bring them to justice", while Binance also said it was working with the FBI.

While Binance said its US-facing exchange was unaffected, the US would still have jurisdiction to investigate the hack and help track down stolen funds - an effort which generally involves a global law enforcement effort.

The hack also involved some US-linked assets, such as stablecoins Tether and Circle â cryptocurrencies pegged to the dollar.

Binance told its users "your money is safe, and we're covering every loss".

It had initially suspended all deposits and withdrawals - but then said in an update that "deposits remain unaffected".

Binance's co-CEO Richard Teng said: "This was not carried out by one person. Our initial investigation suggested that the security incident may have involved a compromised laptop, however our subsequent investigation has uncovered additional points of breach."

Teng also describes how the hackers used "a self-destruct mechanism that destroyed tooling and traces as they went, making it more difficult for us to trace and investigate".

Initially, the company had indicated the breach "may have started with a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop" but later statements have pointed to a broader breach.

In a later update, the firm set it had "set aside" more than $6.34B in company funds to cover the stolen assets and was "actively buying" a range of crypto tokens.

The Binance hack spooked cryptocurrency markets on Friday. The price of Bitcoin was down 5% to $76,800 in Friday afternoon trading in New York.

Cryptocurrency exchange Coinbase was down 12% as investors also reacted to earnings results in which the firm reported revenue slightly below expectations. Robinhood, a financial services company that trades in cryptocurrency, was down 7.8%.

Binance, which was founded in China in 2017, allows users to trade digital assets and is estimated to have more than 300 million registered users worldwide.

The company said in 2024 that it had more than $160bn in assets under its custody.

A lot of the fear right now from Binance users - and the crypto industry at large - is whether or not Binance will actually be capable of covering this enormous financial loss.

The company has said repeatedly that customer funds will be covered and that the firm is strong enough to weather the storm.

But crypto history watchers have been pointing out online that this is exactly the kind of message that FTX CEO Sam Bankman-Fried gave before his crypto exchange collapsed in 2022, leading to huge losses for customers and the industry.

Several experts have warned customers not to regard Binance's promise to cover losses as legally binding and said they may need to pursue litigation to recover them.

Daniel Tunkel, of the London-based Edwin Coe law firm, said that would "come down to what [customers] have been promised in their contracts", and "where Binance can be sued for non-performance, if it comes to it".

Binance has been subject of regulatory scrutiny around the world.

Meanwhile, AI-powered investigations company TRM Labs said that several elements of the attack resembled earlier North Korean-linked incidents, including the Bybit hack.

In the last five years the crypto industry has been beset by enormous losses from hackers.

In nearly all cases investigators have blamed hackers from North Korea, pointing to the infamous Lazarus Group - also know as TraderTraitor. The names refer to clusters of cyber criminals accused of working directly for the regime.

Chainalysis, a crypto analytics firm, said it had "high confidence" that a North Korean is linked to the hack.

North Korea is an impoverished country with very little industry and heavy international sanctions and experts say the regime uses cyber crime as a way to bring money in.

North Korea has always denied this, but the wealth of evidence from cryptocurrency flow analysis and insider interviews from defectors is compelling.

It makes North Korea likely to be the only nation that uses hacking as a way to raise public funds.

[a1] Guardian World <https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/30/how-crypto-hacking-funds-isolated-north-korea-nuclear-programme> (FULL TEXT)
Cryptocurrency theft is estimated to generate half of the country’s foreign currency income

North Korea plays by its own rules. The isolated, secretive and autocratic country is largely cut off from the world’s economic system – but when it comes to generating foreign earnings it relies on some of the world’s most sophisticated hacking.

Experts, such as TRM Labs, quickly attributed the extraordinary $6.3bn (£4.7bn) Binance cryptocurrency theft to TraderTraitor, an elite North Korean hacking unit that has also been known as the Lazarus Group. Though the link may not be proven yet, the pattern of behaviour is familiar.

North Korea has a history of cryptocurrency theft dating back to 2017, focusing on the sector’s relatively lax security controls as a way of stealing money and generating revenues for its military, including its nuclear weapons programme.

Kim Jong-un, its dictatorial ruler, controls a stockpile of about 50 nuclear warheads, and has been supplying troops and weapons to help Russia in its war in Ukraine. But North Korea is also extremely poor: 40% of the population are undernourished according to the UN and nearly one in five of its children are stunted.

A UN panel of experts, reporting to the security council, said in the spring of 2024 that it was reviewing “58 suspected cyber-attacks” by North Korea “on cryptocurrency-related companies between 2017 and 2023” worth $3bn.

The panel said the cash was estimated to generate half of North Korea’s foreign currency income. One unnamed country went further with its estimate: the stolen billions fund “40% of the weapons of mass destruction programmes”.

The suspected crypto hacks amounted to as many as 17 in 2023 alone: thefts valued at $750m, and disturbingly, the sums involved are only increasing. The world’s largest previous crypto hack took place in February 2025, when $1.5bn was stolen from the Bybit exchange – the work, the FBI said, of TraderTraitor.

Ciaran Martin, a former head of the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre, argues that North Korea appears to have targeted crypto because traditional banks have developed sophisticated defences that the fast-growing and lightly regulated sector has yet to match.

“Over the last decade, hackers have walked off with nine- or 10-figure sums from crypto hacks on more than 30 occasions. The number of traditional banks who have suffered a loss of more than $100m as a result of a cyber-attack in the same period is zero or one,” Martin said.

North Korea was behind one of the last large cyber thefts on a traditional bank – an attack on Bangladesh Bank in February 2016, where North Korean hackers tried to illicitly move $951m from an account the bank had with the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, taking advantage of time differences between the two.

A piece of luck prevented most of the money from being sent to an intermediate account in the Philippines. The corresponding bank was based on Jupiter Street, which shares its first word with the name of a sanctioned Iranian ship, so most of the transfers were held up. In the end, $81m was stolen.

The isolated state has long been accused of engaging in criminal activities to survive, including the forgery of high-quality $100 bills, known as “supernotes” in the 1990s, and the production and trafficking of crystal meth for export in the 2000s.

The UK has been rarely targeted. In 2019, North Korean hackers did try to take control of cash machines linked to 56 financial institutions to make them pay out sums well in excess of normal. British officials spotted what was afoot, alerted the banks and the hack was quietly suppressed with no media coverage.

The irony is that the internet in North Korea is severely restricted, with access denied to the general population. Maths and science are pushed at school, however, and at university some of the best students are recruited into hacker groups, such as Lazarus, operating under the auspices of the country’s Reconnaissance General Bureau spy agency.

Hacking is closely tied to the all-powerful state. In December 2014, it emerged, to the embarrassment of Sony Pictures, that film stars Jennifer Lawrence and Amy Adams were paid less than their male counterparts in American Hustle. It was one of thousands of embarrassing emails leaked from the Hollywood studio through a hack.

The attack, by North Korea’s Lazarus Group, was conducted because Sony had funded a film, The Interview: a Seth Rogen comedy that depicted the assassination of Kim Jong-un. Amid the chaos of the revelations, Sony tried to cancel the movie, then had to U-turn on its retreat, releasing it online and to a handful of cinemas, after being criticised for caving in to North Korean pressure.

# Biggest hack in history: Cryptocurrency giant Binance loses $6.3 billion

Issued on: 30/10/202

[a4] NPR World <https://www.npr.org/2026/10/30/g-s1-150902/blockchain-intelligence-firms-link-north-korea-to-6-3b-hack-on-binance> (FULL TEXT)
FILE - The Binance logo is seen on a smartphone, Feb. 28, 2023, in Marple Township, Pa. Matt Slocum/AP hide caption

A U.S.-based blockchain intelligence firm says a North Korean unit was likely behind the hack that impacted more than $6 billion on Binance, the world's biggest cryptocurrency exchange.

Binance said it worked with blockchain partners to freeze roughly $3 billion in affected assets. It said Friday that it was beginning to recover assets for users.

The cryptocurrency exchange said deposits were unaffected, while withdrawals have been suspended and are expected to resume in the next day or two. It said it will cover all losses.

The company said on X that an initial investigation suggested the incident may have involved a compromised laptop, but that a subsequent investigation uncovered additional "points of breach."

TRM, in a post on its website Thursday, attributed the attack to TraderTraitor, which it described as North Korea's elite hacking unit. "We make this judgment with high confidence," the firm said.

"An attacker moved approximately USD 6.36 billion out of Binance wallets on ten blockchains, most of it in about 20 minutes," TRM said. That would make the hack the largest cryptocurrency theft on record, it said.

In February last year, another cryptocurrency exchange, Bybit, suffered a $1.5 billion hit when it was hacked. The FBI said North Korea and TraderTraitor were responsible for the hack.

"Several features of this attack match earlier North Korea-linked heists, including the Bybit hack," TRM said.

Blockchain analysis company Chainalysis also believed the Binance hack was "likely done by DPRK group TraderTraitor," its co-founder and CEO Jonathan Levin wrote on X. DPRK stands for the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, North Korea's official name.

Asked about the potential ties to North Korea, a Binance spokesperson said only that the company is "working closely with our law enforcement partners to confirm the identity of the criminals and bring them to justice."

Experts say North Korea has turned to crypto hacking and other illicit cyber activities as a source of badly needed foreign currency to support its troubled economy and fund its nuclear program in the wake of tough U.N. sanctions and other difficulties.

In December 2022, South Korea's spy agency said North Korean hackers had stolen an estimated 1.5 trillion won ($1.1 billion) in cryptocurrency and other virtual assets in the past five years, more than half of it in 2022 alone. It said North Korea's capacity to steal digital assets was considered among the best in the world.

Changpeng Zhao, also known as CZ, founded Binance in 2017. In 2023, he pleaded guilty to a felony linked to failing to prevent money laundering on the platform, and stepped down as the company's CEO. U.S. President Donald Trump pardoned Zhao in 2025.

Zhao posted Thursday on X that "User funds are safe!" but added that the theft "does look like a hack on Binance." He added that while he doesn't manage Binance anymore, "as a shareholder, I expect all user funds will be fully covered."

The event, meanwhile, also raises questions about why stronger approval controls were not in place, said Kok Tin Gan, cofounder and CEO of cybersecurity firm NyxLab.

"This looks like one signature that gave the attacker a never-expiring, full-access token to a Binance hot wallet," he said, referring to cryptocurrency wallets that hold users' "private keys," or unique authorizations, digitally and which are connected to the internet.

Binance said on X it had "found no evidence of any wallet private key leak" and that the company would release more technical details when its probe is complete.

TEL AVIV, Israel — Israel's election has produced a political deadlock, leaving neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu nor his main challenger, Gadi Eisenkot, with a clear path to forming a government.

The final results announced Friday give opposition parties more seats than Netanyahu's right-wing bloc. But Eisenkot, a center-right former military chief of staff, cannot reach a governing majority without the support of predominantly Arab parties — an option Eisenkot's allies repeatedly ruled out during the campaign.

Netanyahu's Likud party received the most votes — winning 27 seats — but his bloc lost the majority it had before the election. The coalition of right-wing, far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties that supported his government won a combined 53 seats. At least 61 seats are needed for a majority in Israel's 120-seat parliament, known as the Knesset.

Here is what happens next and the possible paths to a new Israeli government.

In the next few days, Israel's president will consult with the leaders of every party elected to parliament. Each party will recommend the party leader it believes should be given the first opportunity to form a coalition.

The president is not required to choose the leader of the largest party. Instead, the president selects the candidate considered most likely to assemble a governing coalition.

That candidate is given 28 days to secure the support of at least 61 lawmakers. The president may grant an extension of up to 14 days. If the effort fails, the president can give another candidate an opportunity to form a government.

The process could take weeks, or months, or end without anyone assembling a majority.

Based on the election results, Eisenkot appears more likely than Netanyahu to receive the largest number of recommendations from newly elected lawmakers.

But that does not mean Eisenkot — a relative newcomer to politics — will be able to form a coalition. The group of parties he campaigned with won 48 seats.

If his bloc allies with Arab-led parties, which won a historic 19 seats, that would form a majority. Palestinian citizens of Israel represent 21% of the population.

But Eisenkot and others in his bloc have promised not rely on Arab parties' support. Eisenkot has said the Arab parties' positions on Hamas and the war in Gaza prevent him from governing with them — some have refused to endorse the military goal of eliminating Hamas, or condemning it as a terrorist organization. Other leaders in his bloc made similar statements before and after the election.

Netanyahu faces a different version of the same problem. His traditional coalition partners do not have enough seats to return him to power.

One possibility is a unity government bringing together Netanyahu and Eisenkot.

Israeli and some U.S. media outlets have reported that President Trump may favor such an outcome — although NPR has not independently confirmed the reports. A unity government would likely include more moderate parties from Israel's political center and right.

President Trump endorsed Netanyahu in the lead-up to the election, and later boasted that he thought it turned the tide in the election.

Such a coalition could make it easier for Israel to work with the Trump administration on its regional priorities, including the reconstruction of Gaza and a potential normalization agreement with Saudi Arabia.

But Eisenkot has repeatedly pledged not to serve in a government led by Netanyahu. The two leaders would also have to resolve questions about who would serve as prime minister and whether the position would rotate between them.

A second possibility is an Eisenkot government supported by Arab parties.

Together, Eisenkot's bloc and the Arab parties would command a comfortable parliamentary majority of more than 67 seats. But such a coalition remains unlikely because Eisenkot and his allies have repeatedly rejected it.

Support from the Arab parties would not necessarily require them to join the government. They could support a minority government from outside the coalition. But that arrangement would still contradict the campaign promises made by Eisenkot and several of his potential partners.

Israel's president has urged political leaders to avoid sending the country back to the polls, particularly after an election that drew the highest turnout in decades. Israeli President Isaac Herzog said he expects party leaders "to come with an open mind" in their discussions with him.

But if no candidate can assemble a majority, another election remains possible.

While Ukrainian territory lies just 3 miles (5km) from Oleshky, evacuees will need to travel to the southern Russian city of Anapa and on to Belarus, and then drive across the border into Ukraine’s Chernihiv region.

Davyd Arakhamia, a lawmaker with the Ukrainian president Volodymyr Zelenskyy’s Servant of the People party – who was part of the delegation to Oleshky – confirmed the evacuation of some people who were travelling to Anapa.

“From there, they will fly by plane to Belarus, and then travel by bus to their homes,” he said.

Kyrylo Budanov, the head of Ukraine’s presidential office, wrote in an online post: “At this moment, the humanitarian operation is under way in Oleshky.” Budanov is not part of the delegation in Oleshky.

While earlier reports had suggested more than 500 people wanted to move to Ukrainian territory, it was unclear how many people travelled in the convoy.

Kyiv’s delegation reached the city late on Friday morning to prepare for the evacuation, with footage on social media showing tearful residents embracing delegation members as they gathered in heavy rain to wait for the main evacuation convoy to arrive. Several hundred people, mainly older people, have chosen to stay.

Asked if she was packed and ready to leave by a member of the Ukrainian delegation, a woman who identified herself as “Anna” was shown on video saying: “Yes, we are on bus 20, my son is on bus 17. To Ukraine.”

Oleshky’s population has declined to about 2,000 since the star of the war, according to authorities and volunteers, and many of those who stayed have given harrowing accounts of risking their lives travelling along mine-strewn roads to get food and water.

Some say they have survived on a cup of rice or buckwheat a day, sometimes with a tomato, cucumber or wild plants added.

“We’ve been through so much here in these five years,” one woman said in a report broadcast on Ukrainian TV. Another older woman, preparing to evacuate, said: “If we had our health, maybe we’d stay there a bit longer, but that’s it. We just want to be with our children.”

Ksenia Arkhipova, a volunteer who previously lived in Oleshky and remains in contact with people there, said large numbers of Russian military personnel were in the city.

“They are currently checking empty houses and, in some cases, moving into them,” she told the Associated Press. “They are also pressuring local residents to evacuate to Russia, rather than to Ukraine,” she added.

The convoy arrived under a ceasefire in the area, which the US first lady, Melania Trump, claimed to have helped broker – a rare example of cooperation between the warring sides.

On Friday, the Kremlin, however, described it as a special case that was “difficult to extrapolate” to the wider search for a peace settlement.

Oleshky was seized by Russian troops during the first days of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022.

It has suffered extensive damage and was badly flooded after the destruction of the huge upriver Kakhovka dam in June 2023.

“The problem is that a lot of people are staying,” one woman told Ukraine’s human rights ombudsman, Dmytro Lubinets, who was on the ground to coordinate the operation with his Russian counterpart, Yana Lantratova.

“I’ve been told that ‌it’s their own choice,” Lubinets replied. “At the very least, we’ll do what we can to ensure that somehow humanitarian aid gets through.”

He added: “This is the first time since 2022 that a ceasefire has been established across such an extensive stretch of the frontline and has been maintained for ​such a significant duration.”

"There are different ways to commit murder," journalist and co-founder of the paper Dmitry Muratov tells me. "They chose to strangle us."

In 2021, Muratov was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for efforts "to safeguard freedom of expression, a precondition for democracy and lasting peace".

The award didn't stop the Russian authorities from designating him a 'foreign agent' in 2023. The label is often used to punish critics of the government.

Over the years Novaya Gazeta had developed a reputation for fearless investigative reporting on subjects such as corruption, human rights violations and abuse of power by officials and bureaucrats - dangerous topics in Russia.

The paper says that six colleagues were killed for "fulfilling their professional and civic duty". Those journalists include Anna Politkovskaya, who was shot dead in the stairwell of her apartment block twenty years ago this month. She had written extensively on the wars in Chechnya.

As I walk down a corridor, I spot the office Anna had used. Following her killing, the room and all the items in it were preserved and became a museum in her memory. In a statement on its website announcing the closure, Novaya Gazeta launched a stinging attack on the Russian authorities.

# Russia’s Novaya Gazeta to cease publication amid Kremlin crackdown

Issued on: 30/10/2026 - 17:58

Share



[a2] Guardian World <https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/oct/30/novaya-gazeta-russia-independent-newspaper-to-close> (FULL TEXT)
Independent outlet, whose former editor Dmitry Muratov won Nobel prize, cites intolerable pressure from authorities

Novaya Gazeta, one of Russia’s best-known independent media outlets, has announced it will permanently close next month. The newspaper, whose former editor-in-chief Dmitry Muratov won the Nobel peace prize in 2021, cited intolerable pressure, including being stripped of its newsroom, its donation platforms and its legal right to publish.

“This is not just our own story; it is the story of a country and a society that are being systematically crushed,” wrote its editor-in-chief, Sergei Sokolov, on the newspaper’s website.

Launched in the 1990s and for many years partly funded by the late Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, Novaya made its name through dogged, long-form reporting on corruption, rights abuses and social issues. Over the years, six of its journalists have been assassinated, including Anna Politkovskaya, who was gunned down in the stairwell of her Moscow apartment block in 2006.

While Novaya always swam against a tide of repression, the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 made independent reporting almost impossible and decimated the Russian media landscape. Novaya was one of the last critical voices still operating from inside the country.

“For nearly five years of ***, we managed to keep working while being completely encircled by the security services. Now it’s over. We have found ourselves in a ‘kill zone’,” wrote Sokolov. The stars were used in place of the word “war”, as under Russian law, journalists must refer to the war in Ukraine as a “special military operation” or face consequences.

The newspaper’s first response to the invasion was to put out a special bilingual Russian-Ukrainian edition in the days after Vladimir Putin launched the invasion. It described the war as “madness” on its front page. Soon after, the Kremlin implemented draconian new censorship laws, which forced media outlets to publish only the Russian official narrative about the conflict.

“We said it’s not journalism and we’re not going to do that,” said Nadezhda Prusenkova, the head of Novaya’s press service. The newspaper published blank white spaces instead of euphemisms and false information, to provide a visible reminder of the censorship laws.

By the end of March 2022, Novaya decided to stop publishing its print edition altogether, but the threats and pressure only increased. In April that year, Muratov was doused with red paint while on a train, an attack that damaged his eyesight and that US officials say was carried out on the orders of Russian intelligence agencies. In September that year, the newspaper’s official licence was taken away and soon after their website was blocked in Russia.

Novaya’s journalists, long used to operating in a hostile environment, found ways to circumvent the bans. Many left Russia, and a number of exiled former employees set up Novaya Gazeta Europe, a legally distinct outlet. Muratov and a core team stayed behind and continued to publish a series of magazines with a print run of 999 copies, taking advantage of a law that means magazines with under 1,000 copies do not require an official licence. Novaya’s website continued running, too, although the Russian state hit the outlet with various new legal threats.

“The fact Novaya kept working for this four and a half years after everything that happened was a miracle, but it was not just a miracle, it was also the daily choice of people who continued to go to work even when it was very scary,” said Elena Kostyuchenko, a former Novaya journalist who spent 17 years at the newspaper. “Today I feel very inspired by my colleagues, but also very painful and sad for them, and for all of us.”

In recent months, Prusenkova said the pressure had become intolerable. In April, the journalist Oleg Roldugin was arrested and charged with “unlawful access to computerised information”. He is under house arrest awaiting trial and last month Sokolov’s home was searched as part of the case.

This month, the newspaper’s landlords informed Novaya staff that they must vacate their longstanding office premises in central Moscow by 9 November. The newsroom has been the hub of the newspaper for decades and houses a memorial museum to Politkovskaya in the former office of the murdered journalist. Prusenkova said the exhibits would be taken down and put into storage, for now.

On top of the eviction notice, all the outlet’s donation platforms have been shut down, leaving the paper without any source of funding. The court case, combined with the eviction and the lack of funds, gave the newspaper no choice but to close, said staff.

“Sometimes it happens that your enemies win. At this moment in time, it seems like they have won, and we have no options left,” said Prusenkova. But she said it was probable Novaya’s journalists would try to find some way to continue their work. “After all, we don’t know how to do anything else,” she said
