At the editorial offices of Novaya Gazeta, they are packing up and shutting down.

Everywhere I look there are cardboard boxes and plastic bags. Everything must go.

For thirty years this has been Russia's most prominent independent newspaper, featuring investigative journalism fiercely critical of those in power.

Now Novaya Gazeta is being evicted from its home of three decades. The paper also says it's been blocked from receiving donations.

"There are different ways to commit murder," journalist and co-founder of the paper Dmitry Muratov tells me. "They chose to strangle us."

"First, they blocked our websites and banned the paper edition. Then they revoked our media licence. One by one we were declared 'foreign agents', and our journalists were attacked.

"Now we can't earn any more money. The banks are refusing to accept donations from our readers. In our country there are political repressions going on. Those have reached us."

In 2021, Muratov was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for efforts "to safeguard freedom of expression, a precondition for democracy and lasting peace".

The award didn't stop the Russian authorities from designating him a 'foreign agent' in 2023. The label is often used to punish critics of the government.

Over the years Novaya Gazeta had developed a reputation for fearless investigative reporting on subjects such as corruption, human rights violations and abuse of power by officials and bureaucrats - dangerous topics in Russia.

The paper says that six colleagues were killed for "fulfilling their professional and civic duty". Those journalists include Anna Politkovskaya, who was shot dead in the stairwell of her apartment block twenty years ago this month. She had written extensively on the wars in Chechnya.

As I walk down a corridor, I spot the office Anna had used. Following her killing, the room and all the items in it were preserved and became a museum in her memory. Since the eviction order, most of the objects have already been removed. All that's left is the desk and, lying on top, a portrait of Anna.

In a statement on its website announcing the closure, Novaya Gazeta launched a stinging attack on the Russian authorities.

"They've killed us," it starts. "The newspaper is no more. The Russian authorities and Russian special services have forced the paper to stop its work."

The publication claims it is the victim of "a coordinated act" and blames "the repressive state apparatus".

Since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the pressure on independent journalism and independent Russian journalists has been growing.

Soon after the start of what the Kremlin calls the 'special military operation', new laws were adopted in Russia. Their aim: to punish dissent and silence criticism of the war and of those in power.

Earlier this year there was a police search of Novaya Gazeta's editorial office. A veteran investigative journalist Oleg Roldugin was arrested and charged with "unlawful access to computerised information".

In connection with the case the newspaper's current editor-in-chief Sergei Sokolov was recently taken in for police questioning and his home was searched.

Despite increasing pressure, Novaya Gazeta managed to continue operating, albeit in a reduced form. Loyal readers used VPNs, virtual private networks, to circumvent official blocking of the website. But the loss of its licence and other restrictions were a major blow to the publication.

"We are in the middle of a terrible catastrophe," Mr Muratov tells me. "Different dictatorships in the world seek chaos. They use chaos to rule their countries and peoples and to strengthen their personal power.

"I don't know what will happen next. I do know that we will do everything to stay true to our values: the human right to life. Human rights override the rights of states."

In an increasingly authoritarian Russia, it's also a sign of the times.
