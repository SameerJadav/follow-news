In dig at Ontario premier Doug Ford, Trump calls him less impressive than late brother Rob Ford, Toronto mayor caught on video smoking crack

As we mentioned earlier, Donald Trump quickly got personal in response to criticism of his new tariffs on Canadian imports from the conservative premier of Ontario, Doug Ford, by comparing him unfavorably to his late brother, Rob Ford, a former rightwing mayor of Toronto.

Writing on social media, Trump called the Ontario leader a “less charismatic, intelligent, and overall unimpressive” version of his brother. What makes the US president’s enthusiastic praise for the late Rob Ford is that he was best known outside Canada for one thing: having been caught on video smoking crack while serving as mayor.

While the video was not made public until after Rob Ford’s death in 2016, he admitted in 2013 that he had smoked crack, “probably in one of my drunken stupors” in 2012.

The same year, the Ford brothers met Trump at the opening of a new Trump International Hotel and Tower in Toronto.

During Trump’s successful campaign for the Republican nomination for president in 2016, Doug Ford expressed delight that Trump had used one of his brother’s slogans.

“It’s a real tribute and a real honour,” Doug Ford told the Toronto Sun.

“Full disclosure, I like Trump,” he added. “He’s not part of the Washington establishment or the Washington elites ... I wouldn’t underestimate him.”

By last year, Doug Ford’s affection for Trump had waned so much that, as Ontario’s leader, he paid to have a television ad against Trump’s tariffs, featuring anti-tariff comments from Ronald Reagan, broadcast during the World Series, which infuriated Trump.

New poll shows record low support for war in Iran

A new Reuters/Ipsos poll shows that public approval for the war with Iran has dropped to its lowest level since the conflict’s early days at the end of February. Now, only 31% of Americans surveyed support military action in Iran, which is down from 34% earlier this month.

Notably, fewer Americans who identify as Republican support the war, according to the poll. That number now sits at 69%, compared to 77% in March.

This comes at a time when Donald Trump’s own approval rating hovers at a new low of 33%, according to recent polling.

All US immigration officers working for a key anti-fraud unit have been reassigned by Donald Trump’s administration to focus on screening South African refugees and alleged “unlawful” voters.

Fraud Detection and National Security Directorate (FDNS) immigration officers have been shifted full-time on to two “high-priority” initiatives, according to internal correspondence seen by the Guardian, leaving US Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) unable to provide officers to train asylum officers in “the near future”.

USCIS is inside the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), which has led the president’s immigration crackdown during his second term. The processing of asylum claims has largely ground to a halt during Trump’s administration, leaving many seeking asylum in limbo.

“All FDNS IOs [immigration officers] have been reassigned full-time to one of two high-priority USCIS initiatives (South African refugee screening and unlawful voter screening),” an internal message, sent on Monday, said. “Because of this, we will not have the ability to assign IO’s [immigration officers] to the training portfolio for the near future.”

USCIS did not immediately respond to a request for comment.

FDNS is the USCIS unit responsible for detecting fraud and national security concerns in immigration benefit applications, including asylum claims. Its officers work with asylum offices to identify cases requiring further investigation, refer matters to law enforcement, and help asylum officers identify potential fraud and other red flags.

The reassignment marks a significant diversion of FDNS personnel. The message addresses only the agency’s ability to provide officers for asylum officer training; it does not say whether routine FDNS screening of pending asylum applications has also slowed or stopped.

A disruption to training would limit specialized instruction for asylum officers, and a broader slowdown in casework screening would have more direct consequences for pending applications. The Guardian has not independently confirmed whether routine asylum screening has been affected.

Bessent evades questions about whether sweeping secondary sanctions will include China

Notably, during his press conference today, Scott Bessent didn’t specify which countries would be targeted by secondary sanctions, even when asked by reporters whether China – the largest importer of Iranian oil – would be impacted.

While Bessent said that “quiet diplomacy” is the best way to engage with countries who still maintain trade relationships with Iran, this latest move is an attempt to show that “no one is above” sanctions.

“We know who they are, they know who they are, so when the hammer of US treasury actions falls upon them they will have no one to blame but themselves,” Bessent said.

He described the new wave of sanctions as “economic asphyxiation” of the Iranian regime and “no one should test our resolve”.

The treasury secretary also said he would not “name names” about the world leaders that Donald Trump is in touch with when it comes to ending business with Iran.

Bessent also added: “I’m not going to set timelines, but we do not have infinite patience here.”

Republicans pushback on Trump policy to temporarily pause import tariffs on foreign beef

Following Donald Trump’s announcement that there would be a temporary pause on tariffs to allow for more than 300,000 metric tons of foreign beef to be imported to the US, a number of Republicans have pushed back on the latest trade policy – arguing that it stands to negatively impact American ranchers who are already feeling the burden of higher costs.

The president has said the move, announced on Friday, would “reduce prices for Americans while giving space for our Great American Beef Herd to grow again”, however, several GOP lawmakers claim this is a short-term fix for a chronic problem, namely a 75-year low in US cattle supply.

“I absolutely want to see those prices come down,” said Congresswoman Ashley Hinson, the Republican nominee in a competitive US Senate race in Iowa. “But I think this isn’t the way to do that.”

In an interview with Fox News on Sunday, Hinson added: “I think we need to focus on letting our cattle men grow their herd … it takes some time and it takes some resources, and so I think we should be letting market conditions to do what they do – demand for beef is still really high.”

Other Republicans in states with sizeable cattle farming industries also criticized the president’s recent decision. “Short term policy shifts do not equal long term solutions,” said Pete Ricketts, a GOP Senator from Nebraska and frequent ally of the Trump. “Flooding the market with lower quality beef compromises Nebraska farmers and ranchers. They should be enabled to grow herd sizes and meet consumer demand.”

Another Trump loyalist, Senator Tim Sheehy of Montana, said that while the president’s “heart is in the right place on wanting lower prices for the American people”, the action would actually make it more difficult for American ranchers to rebuild their herd.

Aside from beef, other Republicans criticized the Trump administration’s ongoing trade war with Canada over the weekend. Senator Susan Collins, a Republican from Maine who is facing a re-election battle this year, called the “on-again/off-again” trade talks between the US and Canada would lead to “higher costs, risk, and uncertainty” for businesses in Maine – which borders Canada.

“Ever since the tariffs were first announced, I have heard from many businesses, farmers, and lobstermen concerned about the cost of navigating the tariffs, the lack of domestic supply chains, and the likely imposition of retaliatory tariffs by Canada,” Collins said in a statement after trade talks broke down over the weekend, urging “both sides to return to the negotiating table”.
