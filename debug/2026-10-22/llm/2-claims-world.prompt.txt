Ukraine's capital has come under a new missile attack, hours after Russia said it was "ready in principle" to resume peace talks and the US president said the world was "the closest we've been" to ending the war.

Several houses were hit on Wednesday night, injuring six people, Kyiv Mayor Vitaliy Klitschko said.

The attack came amid a new diplomatic push led by Donald Trump's envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. Witkoff met Russia's President Vladimir Putin in the city of St Petersburg on Wednesday - a day after meeting Ukraine's leader.

Three-way talks were paused in February, after which Russia stepped up attacks as Ukraine ran out of Western-supplied interceptors to defend against missiles.

Speaking on Wednesday, Trump said: "The other day I was talking to Vladimir Putin about settling his war with Ukraine, which I think we're getting closer to, by the way, maybe now the closest we've been."

The US president has so far failed to bring the Russia-Ukraine war to an end despite his repeated comments that he would be able to achieve this.

Rosenberg: US diplomatic push offers little hope for imminent Ukraine peace

Trump's special envoy Witkoff went to St Petersburg after meeting Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelensky as well as the leaders of Norway and Finland in the Norwegian capital, Oslo, on Tuesday.

It is not clear what specific proposals Witkoff brought to Putin, but Zelensky said after the talks that "strong" security guarantees for Ukraine and energy ceasefire proposals had been discussed.

Putin's aide Yuri Ushakov said their ideas were not very "useful".

Top officials from Russia, Ukraine and the US have held several rounds of talks aimed at ending the war.

Russia blamed last month's "massive" Ukrainian strikes during its general elections for their disruption.

Putin launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, and Moscow currently controls about 20% of Ukrainian territory.

Speaking to reporters after Wednesday's talks in St Petersburg, Ushakov suggested that a new round could be held around 10 November in Abu Dhabi, the capital of the United Arab Emirates.

He stressed that the talks must focus on "building a security system for Russia and Ukraine after the end of the conflict."

Ushakov also told reporters that Putin reiterated to Witkoff that Russian troops were advancing everywhere across the vast front line and had a "strategic initiative".

Ukraine and its European allies have disputed Putin's claim, pointing to a virtual stalemate on the front.

On Wednesday, Zelensky said he would accept a deal that would freeze the war along the current front line.

"We call it 'ceasefire' or 'stop killings' like President Trump called it. Freeze and stop. And of course this is minimum what we need from Russia today," he told Fox News, adding that security guarantees for Ukraine could be discussed after this.

Witkoff described the latest talks as "constructive", while Kushner, who was participating via a video link, said: "There is still hard work ahead, but we have a real opportunity to move beyond this war and build a framework for lasting peace, security, and prosperity for generations to come."

The talks were held amid a growing row between Ukraine and the US.

Trump has said it is time for Ukraine to get a new president, and most recently "for somebody else to negotiate on behalf of Ukraine".

He has blamed Ukraine's attacks on Russian oil refineries for fuel prices which have been rising since the start of the US-led war on Iran.

He was also heavily criticised by Democratic and some Republican lawmakers after lifting sanctions on Russia - allowing it to release tonnes of diesel "to the American and global marketplace".

That was despite signing legislation to authorise fresh US sanctions and tariffs on nations that import Russian oil and gas just weeks earlier.

Zelensky - and Ukraine's European allies - criticised Trump's move.

Last week, Kyiv accused Washington of withholding critical intelligence to warn Ukrainians about incoming Russian ballistic missile launches, resulting in many civilian deaths.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio on Tuesday rejected such accusations as "scandalous", and Zelensky said after the Oslo talks that intelligence sharing "works now".

Ukraine's military has also complained of speed restrictions to the US satellite internet network Starlink, which constricted its ability to use drones to conduct strikes against Russia.

US officials have denied having requested such a slowdown and a bipartisan group of US senators has called for an urgent investigation.

Before the Putin-Witkoff talks started, Russia released Stephen Hubbard, a 74-year-old American citizen who had been given a jail sentence of nearly seven years for allegedly fighting on behalf of Ukraine.

Trump said the former teacher was "on his way back home" and "there was no 'swap' necessary", while Witkoff called Putin a "humanitarian" for agreeing the release.

The administration of US President Donald Trump has stepped up diplomatic efforts this autumn to revive stalled negotiations aimed at ending Russia's war in Ukraine.

Washington has increased pressure on Kyiv and its European partners while seeking to draw Moscow back to the negotiating table through a series of concessions, including a deal involving Russian diesel exports to the US and the temporary easing of some sanctions.

Yet the Kremlin appears in no hurry to resume meaningful peace talks. Moscow is ready to hold negotiations with Ukraine at a technical level next month, the Kremlin said after the meeting between US special envoy Steve Witkoff and Russian President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg on November 21.

Putin agreed to resume the peace talks mediated by Washington after November 10, his aide Yury Ushakov said after the meeting. He added that the next round of negotiations could take place in Abu Dhabi and would focus on "building the security system for Russia and Ukraine after the end of the war."

According to his aide, Putin also shared some thoughts with Witkoff on the possible trilateral summit involving US President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Ushakov pointed out that Beijing is already preparing the meeting.

Moscow proposed to hold a three-way meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit, scheduled for November 18-19 in Shenzhen, China, The Wall Street Journal reported earlier this week. According to WSJ sources, Putin hopes to reach an agreement with Trump and Xi on ending the war in Ukraine on terms favorable to Russia.

Moscow's proposal for the three-way summit involving Russia, the United States and China — but excluding Ukraine and the European Union — offers a glimpse of the negotiating format the Kremlin would prefer. Russia insists that Ukraine must be prepared to make territorial concessions before negotiations can move forward. Putin has also repeatedly stressed that he is willing to negotiate only if Russian interests are taken into account.

According to the WSJ, Putin views the proposed gathering in Shenzhen as a potential new Yalta Conference, referring to the 1945 meeting of the leaders of the United States, Great Britain and the Soviet Union to discuss the defeat of Nazi Germany and the postwar order in Europe.

The newspaper reported that Chinese officials had indicated through diplomatic channels that Beijing would not object to the three-way format if Trump agreed to participate. Trump, whose relations with Kyiv have deteriorated significantly in recent weeks, has also expressed openness to such talks.

Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy pointed out during his visit in Oslo on October 20 that talks concerning the future of his country will be acceptable only if Ukraine gets a seat at the negotiating table.

Temur Umarov, a researcher at the Carnegie Russia Eurasia Center in Berlin who specializes in China-Russia relations, is not surprised by the Kremlin's proposal. He told DW that Moscow wants to feel respected by Washington and treated as a major international power.

The previous US administration was unwilling to discuss questions affecting Ukraine's sovereignty with Russia without Kyiv's participation. Now, Umarov said, the Kremlin believes such negotiations may have become possible.

Moscow apparently hopes that any agreement reached at the proposed meeting would prompt the White House to pressure Ukraine and the EU into accepting its terms. But what role would China actually play?

Umarov believes Beijing would participate largely as a symbolic partner, rather than as an active mediator responsible for the outcome. "Beijing has no intention of taking responsibility for Ukraine's future," he said.

Simply attending the summit would not impose any additional obligations on China. "It is hardly surprising that the Kremlin insists on this format," Umarov explained. "Russia wants to demonstrate that it is on an equal footing not only with the United States, but also with China. Yet, objectively speaking, Moscow is not the senior partner in that relationship."

Umarov believes China has little direct interest in such negotiations. Even if the leaders issued a joint statement, Beijing would seek language that was as vague and noncommittal as possible, he said. That would allow China to present almost any subsequent development in the war as consistent with the understanding reached at the summit. For Umarov, such flexibility would be essential to Beijing's participation.

A drone strike in a busy market in the disputed region of Abyei has killed more than 150 people, according to local officials.

South Sudanese officials blamed Sudan's military for the attack, without providing evidence, amid the long-running civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) militia, which is reportedly active in the Abyei area.

The special UN mission in Abyei warned in May that the RSF's presence in the resource-rich region had put residents in additional danger, and risked intensifying the older territorial dispute between Sudan and the world's newest sovereign state, South Sudan.

According to local authorities, the attack hit the busy Amiet Market at around midday on Monday.

Yohana Akol Ngor, the caretaker information minister in Abyei, told Reuters news agency on Tuesday that the death toll had more than doubled, reaching at least 153 compared to a stated figure of 73 the previous day.

He said that Sudan's military was responsible for the attack but did not provide evidence. Akol Ngor said around 8,000 people had been displaced by the bombing.

South Sudan's Foreign Ministry also blamed the Sudanese army and said the drone struck multiple locations around the busy market.

One of these, judging by witness accounts, was a fuel depot — and this led to a large fire that proved difficult to bring under control overnight and was still ablaze on Tuesday.

South Sudan said it had summoned Sudan's ambassador to protest and urged Sudan to "refrain from any actions that endanger civilian lives and property."

Medical charity Medecins Sans Frontieres (MSF), or Doctors Without Borders, said that its staff was treating some 20 wounded people at the nearby Ameth Bek hospital.

"So far we received over 20 patients, including several critical cases. Patients with burns were also admitted," said Dr. Christian Lindauer, MSF's medical activities manager at the hospital.

The oil-rich Abyei region's status was not resolved by the 2005 peace agreement that broadly ended decades of civil war in Sudan.

It was also not settled in 2011 when South Sudan became an independent country.

A referendum was planned but never materialized, stalling over disputes on who should be allowed to vote. This left Abyei in a state of limbo, jointly administered and with an interim UN security force on site for 15 years.

The region's majority Ngok Dinka population favors joining South Sudan, while Misseriya nomads who use its pastures favor Sudan.

Amiet Market, the site of the attack, is an important trading hub for both communities.

The unstable region has also experienced intercommunal violence in recent years between Ngok Dinka and neighboring Twic Dinka communities and is home to other armed militias.

The April 2023 outbreak of fighting between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces paramilitary — which has turned into a conflict with fairly entrenched battle lines, putting Sudan into a de facto state of partition — is increasing tension in the already-strained region.

Abyei borders South Kordofan state in Sudan, which has become a major center of fighting in the war.

Sudan's military alleges that the RSF is also operating out of Abyei. The United Nations Interim Security Force for Abyei (UNISFA) issued a similar statement in May.

Sudan's military and the RSF have both been using drones extensively in the conflict.

"We remain alarmed by the relentless drone attacks across Sudan, as well as the damage they are causing to critical infrastructure — these attacks are taking a devastating toll on communities already struggling with the consequences of more than three years of conflict," UN spokesperson Stephane Dujarric told reporters in New York on Tuesday when asked about the attack.
