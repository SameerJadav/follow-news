India and Uzbekistan have set a target of taking bilateral trade to $5 billion by 2030 and agreed to broaden the range of goods traded between the two countries, remove non-tariff barriers and address investment hurdles as the two sides seek to deepen economic ties.

The target was announced during Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s talks with Uzbekistan President Shavkat Mirziyoyev on Sunday, according to a statement issued by the Prime Minister’s Office. The two leaders also called for a business summit to deepen economic and investment ties.

The two countries agreed to create a joint working group to address non-tariff issues and investment barriers, with a particular focus on key sectors of bilateral trade, according to a joint statement issued during Modi’s two-day state visit to Uzbekistan.

They also directed the relevant ministries to explore the next steps on the proposed Uzbekistan-India Preferential Trade Agreement on a time-bound basis, following the conclusion of a joint feasibility study.

Bilateral trade between India and Uzbekistan stood at $1.3 billion in 2025, up 33.3% from the previous year, according to commerce ministry data.

The leaders welcomed the strong economic partnership between the two countries but noted the “considerable untapped potential” to expand bilateral trade and investment, according to the joint statement.

Both sides agreed to take joint measures to broaden the range of goods traded, remove non-tariff barriers and promote industrial cooperation. They will also support efforts to strengthen manufacturing and export capacities, promote regulatory dialogue and explore mutual recognition arrangements to facilitate faster market access.

India and Uzbekistan will strengthen cooperation in e-commerce and digitalisation of trade procedures, including through digital platforms for small and medium-sized enterprises.

Electrical items, engineering products, automobiles, and pharmaceuticals were identified as sectors with significant potential for greater bilateral trade. The two sides also agreed to strengthen business-to-business ties in these areas.

On investment, the countries agreed to identify mutually beneficial opportunities and establish an institutional mechanism to address investors’ concerns and facilitate ease of doing business.

They will also explore greater cooperation between Uzbekistan’s Investment Promotion Agency and Invest India through joint business forums, roadshows, investment seminars and stakeholder interactions, aimed at increasing two-way investment flows.

The two sides agreed to promote collaboration between Uzbek and Indian companies, leverage complementarities between the two economies and strengthen connectivity between their value-added supply chains.

The Uzbek side also appreciated India’s support for Uzbekistan’s accession to the World Trade Organization. Both sides agreed to work together to preserve the multilateral trading system with the WTO at its core, particularly in the interests of developing countries.

The leaders welcomed the outcomes of the Intergovernmental Commission on Trade, Economic, Scientific and Technical Cooperation and the Uzbekistan-India Business Forums held in June on the sidelines of the Tashkent International Investment Forum and in August in New Delhi.

The two leaders decided to elevate the bilateral Strategic Partnership to a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership. They also agreed to establish a foreign ministers-led mechanism to provide high-level coordination and oversee implementation of agreements and arrangements between the two countries.

The two countries will expand cooperation in critical minerals through joint projects covering geological exploration, mining, mineral processing and integrated value chains, according to the joint statement.

They will explore cooperation in geology, mining, nuclear energy and industrial activities, including mineral-resource processing, production of value-added products and recycling.

The two sides expressed interest in Indian companies participating in the development of prospective mineral deposits in Uzbekistan, while Uzbek enterprises could pursue joint projects in India.

The leaders also expressed interest in expanding cooperation in the peaceful uses of nuclear energy. Separately, the PMO said Modi appreciated progress on a long-term arrangement for uranium supplies from Uzbekistan and reaffirmed the importance of strengthening cooperation in critical minerals.

India and Uzbekistan reaffirmed their commitment to improving transport and logistics connectivity through multimodal transport routes and logistics corridors and to restoring freight transportation to reduce costs.

The two sides will also exchange expertise on advanced road-construction technologies, explore joint projects to modernize road infrastructure and consider environmentally friendly and energy-efficient solutions for Uzbekistan’s public transport sector.

Uzbekistan was the first stop in Modi’s two-nation tour, which will also take him to Kyrgyzstan to attend the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) Summit. This was Modi's fourth visit to Uzbekistan, which he described as being at the heart of India's engagement with Central Asia.

“India and Uzbekistan share a deep convergence of perspectives and aspirations,” Modi told a joint media interaction, speaking in Hindi. “A stronger India-Uzbekistan partnership will be a powerful force for peace, progress and prosperity in Central Asia.”

Mirziyoyev described India as a “reliable strategic partner” and noted that New Delhi’s voice and influence in global affairs has grown. Joint ventures between the two countries have increased seven-fold and there are now 14 direct flights every week, he said.

“We are aiming to advance cooperation in sectors such as energy, culture, critical minerals, pharmaceuticals, jewellery and agriculture,” Mirziyoyev said.

“We will prepare roadmaps for each of these sectors. Specific teams from India will come here, and our teams will travel to India, enabling us to clearly define our objectives in these fields,” he added.

Mirziyoyev said the introduction of a 30-day visa-free regime for Indian citizens will make a significant contribution to developing tourism and business exchanges.

PM Modi pointed out that the close defence and security cooperation between the two sides “reflects the deep mutual trust that underpins our relationship”. The two countries will promote direct linkages, co-production and co-development between their defence industries, he said.

“We believe that terrorism, extremism and separatism pose serious challenges to the entire region,” Modi said. “Our shared resolve to adopt a policy of zero tolerance towards them has been, and will continue to be, unwavering.”

A glimpse at India’s recent history suggests that while some protest movements became successful political parties, others did not. The success of the recent youth movement started by the Cockroach Janta Party (CJP) should not be judged by whether it would become a successful political party nor by its forcing the former Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan to resign. Rather, the success of the CJP movement should be judged by the enthusiastic participation of young students for a cause which concerned them; the success of which has raised confidence in them.

They now believe that they can make the government hear their voices even if it is raised from a non-political platform.

Very often the success of a movement is judged by not only what the movement was able to achieve but whether it was able to become a successful political party or not. The 1979 student’s agitation in Assam led by the All Assam Students Union transformed into a successful political party, the Asom Gana Parishad (AGP), which formed the government in 1985 in Assam. The India against corruption movement also transformed into a political party — the Aam Adami Party (AAP) which came to power in Delhi for the first time in 2013. Not only did the India against corruption movement draw large support, the AAP also enjoyed massive electoral gains; it won 67 and 62 seats out of a total of 70 Assembly seats in Delhi with a 54.3% and 53.5% vote share during the 2015 and 2020 Assembly elections respectively.

In the success story of both the AGP and AAP, the youth played an important role. During the 2015 Assembly elections, the AAP’s vote share among young voters (18-22 years of age) was 63%, almost 10% higher compared to its average vote share. The AAP went on to draw 60% of the votes among young voters (18-22 years) during the 2020 Assembly elections as well — 7% higher compared to its average vote share.

If the CJP does turn into a political party, there is hardly any doubt that the new party will attract young voters, as has been the case with other newly formed parties. The newly formed Tamilaga Vettri Kazhagam (TVK), led by actor-turned politician C. Joseph Vijay, formed the government in Tamil Nadu in the very first election it contested in 2026, replacing the two established formations mainly the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam and the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam. The youth played a very important role in the victory of the TVK. While the overall vote share of the TVK was 34.9%, its share among young voters (18-22) was significant at nearly 70%.

Nationally, the Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) has an advantage among young voters, its vote share being 4% higher amongst voters in the age group of 18-22 years. Therefore, the rise of the CJP may affect the BJP more than any other party.

But the question remains, can the CJP attract young voters across the country?

There have also been successful social movements which have failed as political parties. The movement for a separate State of Uttarakhand resulted in the formation of a new State and a new political party, the Uttarakhand Kranti Dal (UKD). However, it failed to make an electoral impact. During the 2002 election, which was the first election held after the formation of Uttarakhand, the UKD polled only 5.4% votes and won just four Assembly seats. There have been similar groups which have led protest movements, demanding Statehood and the like, but most of them could not become successful political parties.

However, there have been movements led by the youth, with the participation of larger social communities, which resulted in a change of the national government. The Jayaprakash Narayan led anti-emergency movement of 1977 dethroned the powerful Indira Gandhi-led government. Similarly, the anti-corruption movement led by Anna Hazare contributed to the defeat of the Congress-led coalition government in 2014.

It should be of no concern whether the CJP becomes a political party or not as the movement has already been able to achieve some noticeable milestones. It emphasised that issues take importance and not leaders as this movement was largely leaderless without the active support of any political party.

It also gave a large number of people the hope that their voices can be heard if they come together in a sustained manner.

The Indian activist movement dubbing itself the "Cockroach Janta Party (CJP)" shot to national prominence in May by leading weeks-long protests over a scandal involving highly competitive entrance exams.

The student-led campaign put the government under intense political pressure and ended with the resignation of Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan in July.

Now, the CJP is taking aim at India's government schools, organizing volunteer visits to classrooms, documenting conditions and demanding accountability for problems.

Launched on August 15, Independence Day in India, the campaign asks parents, students and volunteers to inspect schools and record basic facilities including toilets, drinking water, electricity, classroom furniture and school buildings. The CJP has said the findings will be used to pressure authorities to make improvements.

The initial target is to audit 100 rural government schools over two months. It has also proposed giving recognition to village leaders who improve their schools.

Unlike the first campaign, which was based on street protests, the school audits require a more sustained form of local activism. Volunteers are expected to work consistently at the local level, document conditions and follow up with officials, parents and communities.

The organization is dealing with different layers of the Indian administration. Responsibility for government schools is divided between state governments, local administrations and elected village bodies. The CJP is therefore set to tackle different rules and political interests across the country.

India has over a million government schools, according to Education Ministry data. They make up nearly 70% of all schools in the country but serve less than half of its 247 million schoolchildren.

That scale has given CJP a vast field for its campaign. But it has also brought a new set of challenges.

The authorities are raising questions regarding rules and locations for the CJP scrutiny, as well as safeguards when children are present.

The response is already varying from state to state. Some authorities have ordered their own inspections, while others have restricted access or taken police action against CJP volunteers.

Authorities in Maharashtra and Rajasthan states have objected to school visits carried out without permission.

In one case, a complaint by school authorities resulted in police action against CJP members after they entered the premises to conduct an audit.

In Rajasthan, restrictions have been imposed on outsiders entering government schools, as well as on filming, interviews and other forms of documentation. CJP volunteers in Rajasthan have also said they were attacked while inspecting a school.

"We are not afraid. We are seeking accountability from the government," said CJP spokesperson Ashutosh Ranka.

In the state of Uttar Pradesh, Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath has ordered officials to conduct on-the-spot checks of school facilities and directed police to register cases over what he called misleading videos about schools and colleges.

"Under the Right to Information Act, any citizen can inspect schools but you need to submit an application. And that makes sense as there has to be consideration for students and teaching," Singh told DW.

"All public scrutiny is legitimate. It is our job to scrutinize. But the scrutiny has to follow process. It has to be even-handed. What applies to one citizen must apply to all," she said.

The CJP maintains government schools are publicly funded institutions and citizens have a legitimate right to ask how they are functioning.

Its volunteers use standardized audit forms to record conditions and send their findings to the CJP, which has said it will use the material to press local administrations to respond.

But critics question whether citizen activists can assume the role of inspectors. There are concerns about whether volunteers have the expertise to assess safety or infrastructure problems and how allegations posted on social media are verified.

Mohandas Pai, an education advocate, supports CJP's focus on government schools but disagrees with its methods.

"I like young people taking interest in school education. It's a broken system, and we need reforms," Pai told DW.

But he objected to large groups entering schools and questioning headmasters and children.

"You can't just barge into a school just because you think you are entitled to do it," Pai said, stressing that "children have to have a safe, secure environment."
