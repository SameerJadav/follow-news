EDMONTON, Alberta — Voters in oil-rich Alberta province overwhelmingly backed remaining in Canada, according to referendum results released Tuesday, delivering a boost to Prime Minister Mark Carney's push for national unity as U.S. President Donald Trump's trade war and annexation threats rally Canadians around their country.

With more than 1.8 million votes counted and results reported from 84% of voting areas and locations, 75% of voters supported remaining in Canada, while 25% backed beginning a process toward separation, according to unofficial results from Elections Alberta.

The referendum was not a direct vote on independence. Approval would have set Alberta on a path toward a future vote on separation from Canada.

"It's a great result for Canada that Albertans have chosen unity," Carney told reporters Tuesday before a Cabinet meeting in Ottawa.

Carney said that the roughly one-quarter of Albertans who voted to begin the separation process did not necessarily want independence, saying only a smaller "hard core" group may actually want to leave Canada.

Some voters said they supported another referendum to express frustration with Ottawa rather than a desire to leave Canada.

"The result is absolutely clear. It's a vast majority in favor of staying in Canada. And now is the time to work together for all Albertans and all Canadians," Carney said.

Carney said he spoke with Alberta Premier Danielle Smith on Tuesday morning about the referendum's results and greater federal-provincial cooperation, including immigration. He said their first priority was supporting victims and families affected by the deadly military training fire at Canadian Forces Base Wainwright.

"It is fair to say that at least a quarter of Albertans feel that Alberta needs to be treated better," said Keith Wilson, a leader of the separatist movement.

Smith welcomed the early results, saying they showed widespread support for remaining in Canada.

"Albertans have voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada," Smith posted on social media.

Smith, who called the referendum amid pressure from separatists within her governing United Conservative Party, or UCP, despite opposing independence, said that she would address the province Tuesday evening about the results and Alberta's future.

"We are a solid country that's united from coast to coast to coast," Thomas Lukaszuk, a former Alberta deputy premier who led the Forever Canadian campaign against separation, told supporters.

The results were delayed after late openings forced more than 100 polling stations to extend voting hours Monday.

Alberta, home to the world's fourth-largest proven oil reserves, is cowboy country and a conservative stronghold whose political culture has long had more in common with the American West than much of Canada. Resentment toward Canada's federal government in Ottawa runs deep, but hasn't traditionally translated into widespread support for independence.

Despite the overwhelming rejection, separatist leaders have said they turned a fringe cause into a movement that won't disappear.

Separatist parties traditionally attracted only 1% or 2% of the vote, former Premier Jason Kenney said. Anger over COVID-19 restrictions helped mobilize activists who later embraced the separatist movement, he said.

Trump's trade war and threats to make Canada the 51st U.S. state have helped Carney rally support in Alberta, where Liberal prime ministers have historically been unpopular.

At a separatist referendum night gathering at an Edmonton restaurant, the crowd fell quiet as results rolled in.

"We're not going anywhere," said Jeff Rath of the Alberta Prosperity Project. "We've dominated the conversation in the last year. We've been educating Albertans on the benefits of independence, and we're going to continue moving forward."

Rath had said he would be thrilled with 30% support. He wants Smith ousted as UCP leader and said the party could splinter, potentially creating a new pro-independence party.

Ian Brodie, a prominent conservative and political scientist at the University of Calgary who served as chief of staff to former prime minister Stephen Harper, dismissed current independence organizers Rath and Mitch Sylvestre as "nobodies."

"Even on a very soft question — whether to hold a referendum on separation — they couldn't gain real traction in the Olds-Didsbury district," said Brodie.

The district elected a Western Canada Concept separatist in a 1982 provincial byelection.
