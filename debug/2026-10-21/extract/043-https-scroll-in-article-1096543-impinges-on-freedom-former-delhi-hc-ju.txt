On October 13, the Delhi Police had detained Cockroach Janta Party Co-Convenors Ashutosh Ranka and Saurav Das for staging a symbolic protest with the national flag in Central Delhi’s Jantar Mantar, the day after it was denotified as a protest site.

The two have since then been in Tihar Jail because an executive magistrate found them to be in breach of their peace bonds. They had signed these bonds after the police detained them at Delhi airport ahead of their protest against Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar on October 10.

Section 126 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita empowers executive magistrates to ask suspects to sign such bonds. Executive magistrates are usually police officers appointed by state governments to decide cases of preventive arrest.

However, lawyers and activists have long complained that the police misuse this provision to appropriate powers that are otherwise vested solely in the judiciary. In 2018, Justice S Muralidhar pulled up the government of Delhi for using the provision to illegally arrest a resident named Narender and send him to judicial custody.

In his judgement, the judge said that such officers could send suspects to custody for no more than seven days. In addition to that, he had categorically mentioned that police officers serving as executive magistrates could not extend suspects’ custody on technical grounds, such as claiming to need more time to verify their paperwork.

That is exactly what was done to Ranka and Das on Monday, when the executive magistrate extended their judicial custody for two more days. Muralidhar told Scroll that there was something “radically wrong” with the provisions that the police had invoked against them.

“The executive is the complainant, the judge and the executioner,” Muralidhar stated. He asserted that the police were bypassing the standard criminal procedure, which would require a first information report to be filed and, eventually, prove that a crime had been committed.

Muralidhar called for the Delhi High Court, which is expected to take up the case on Wednesday, to strike down this provision.

The case of Ranka and Das has put the spotlight on the office of the executive magistrate. You had gone into this issue at length in your 2018 judgement. Could you explain what this office is?

It is basically a peacekeeping kind of a function. Sometimes the executive might, on the basis of credible information, apprehend that there could be a breach of peace by certain persons. In order to ensure that such persons do not do that, a bond is taken from them containing an assurance.

It is a preventive function. It is not meant to be punitive. The entire purpose of preventive custody is only about an apprehension regarding the possible commission of a crime. It is not about the actual commission of a crime. So, a mere apprehension regarding the commission of a crime should not per se lead to sending a person to judicial custody.

But this is a potent weapon in the hands of the executive and has its vestiges in colonial times. It was extensively used by the British since the 19th century. Given the spirit of Articles 21 and 22 of our Constitution [which guarantee the right to life, personal liberty and protection from arbitrary arrest], these provisions were not expected to survive in free India.

There was no reason to keep them in the statute book at all. And yet, not only were they continued, but have been persisted with and used extensively across the country in the eight decades after freedom. They appear to have a robust afterlife.

Executive magistrate sounds like an oxymoron, doesn’t it? You, in fact, wanted them to be called executive officers because they are usually police officers.

What is the purpose of this kind of provision? There is no first information report. It begins with the police giving a complaint to itself. The kalandara [complaint note] is written by a sub-inspector or a constable. It is addressed to the executive magistrate, who is also a policeman, but of a high rank like a deputy commissioner of police.
