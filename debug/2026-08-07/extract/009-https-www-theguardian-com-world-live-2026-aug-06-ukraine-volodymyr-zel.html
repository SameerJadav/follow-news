Ceuta residents 'sad, helpless, anxious' as up to 5,000 migrants remain in the city, president says

Over in Brussels, Ceuta’s conservative president Juan Jesús Vivas is briefing European lawmakers on the situation in the city in the aftermath of last week’s migration crisis with Morocco.

He tells MEPs that the incident amount to “an invasion” defined as “an anomalous or ir regulation occupation of a specific place.”

He says 3,000 to 5,000 people migrants of the 80,000 who he says crossed the border still remain in the city, which “in no way allows us to say that our city has returned to normal.”

“We have a situation where the influx has led to a humanitarian emergency of enormous proportions, compounded by disturbances to public order and a high risk to public safety.”

He warns lawmakers that “our people feel sad, helpless, anxious, fearful and worried, and they have good reason to, because it is inevitable to wonder whether this will happen again at some point.”

Vivas also says that “it has become evident” that “our security and integrity are in the hands of a third country, Morocco, that does not recognise our sovereignty.”

He calls upon Spanish national authorities to “adopt whatever measures necessary to restore the normality,” including “immediate expulsion” of all people who crossed the border irregularly.

He also laments the death of some 100 people who died during the crisis.

Closing summary

Jakub Krupa

And, on that note, we will be closing the blog for today.

  • Ceuta’s conservative president Juan Jesús Vivas told European lawmakers that the Spanish enclave’s residents were left feeling “sad, helpless, anxious” after last week’s storm on the border with Morocco.

  • He said that up to 80,000 irregular migrants crossed the border, and while most later returned to Morocco, 3,000 to 5,000 still remained in the territory (14:21).

  • Some 100 people died during the border crisis, Vivas said.

  • EU migration commissioner Magnus Brunner accepted that the situation “has shaken” European politics and prompted concern about the bloc’s external borders among EU citizens, but insisted the integrity of the travel-free Schengen zone remained “preserved” as he praised Spain’s response to the situation (14:29).

In other news,

  • Lithuanian defence minister warned that Russia could be considering possible “false flag” provocations against Nato countries in the Baltic region to undermine their support for Ukraine (11:18).

  • Seeking to reassure the public, the country’s prime minister, stressed that citizens should not be “additionally concerned” as the threat level remains generally stable (15:45).

Elsewhere,

  • German media reported that one of the Ukrainian planes near where the drone was spotted at Leipzig airport was carrying “military ammunition” transported from France and “likely intended for onward transport” (12:42).

  • Authorities in Hungary (13:24) and Romania (13:48) adopted further energy-saving measures as the countries are still gripped by unprecedented heatwave and drought, impacting their energy generation.

Lithuanian PM tells citizens not to be 'additionally worried' after warnings over possible Russian 'false flag' operation

Meanwhile, Lithuanian prime minister Mindaugas Sinkevičius said there was no reason for Lithuanians to be “additionally worried,” despite earlier warning about a potential Russian “false flag” attack on the Baltics or Poland (11:18).

He acknowledged that “a certain level of threat exists,” but stressed that the country “has faced various hybrid attacks before,” LRT reported.

“The state is preparing and doing its work quietly. We do not explain everything because we do not want unfriendly neighbouring states to know too much,” he said.

He said the growing number of Russia-linked incidents in Europe – specifically referencing the drone incident at Leipzig airport earlier this week – shows that “as [Ukraine] war approaches its climax, there is often desperation.”

‘Morocco feels emboldened’: Trump’s influence in spotlight after Ceuta disaster

Ashifa Kassam

in Madrid

The cosy relationship between Washington and Rabat has long been flaunted by both sides, from Morocco’s announcement this year that it would be the first African state to join Donald Trump’s Board of Peace to the congratulatory message recently sent by Trump to mark King Mohammed VI’s 27 years in power.

The latest example landed this week. As Spain grappled with the aftermath of the sudden influx – and swift return – of more than 70,000 people into Ceuta, Morocco’s monarch confirmed he had named a $1bn highway flanking the disputed territory of Western Sahara after Trump.

The deepening ties have come under renewed scrutiny as questions continue to swirl over what exactly led to the deadly mass border crossing into the tiny Spanish territory in north Africa.

“I think there’s enough evidence to hypothesise or claim that Morocco is behind this,” said Riccardo Fabiani, the north Africa project director for the International Crisis Group. “But there was no clear trigger for this, and this is what is puzzling for everyone.”

While Morocco had probably not “masterminded” the surge into Ceuta, he said, its relationship with the Trump administration – along with the Moroccan king’s desire to keep its claim to Ceuta in the spotlight – may have affected how it responded to fast-spreading online rumours about the Spanish border being thrown open.

Brunner now keeps going through ideas on how to strengthen the bloc’s external borders.

But then he says there is also one “very important” reminder that the EU needs to work with its neighbours, with a comment on Morocco.

“We must cooperate with our neighbours, of course, but as long as control depends on the goodwill of one neighbouring state, we remain … vulnerable. And we know that unless something changes, migrant smugglers will try it again.”

He says the EU needs to enhance cooperation with third countries on returns and readmission, and step up its social media monitoring for any hints of possible attempts to cross the border.

Events in Ceuta have shaken European politics, EU migration commissioner says

EU migration commissioner Magnus Brunner is speaking next.

He begins by saying that overall irregular migration into the EU is being controlled more tightly than in the last decade, with the number of asylum applications going down across the bloc.

But he says this was understandably “overshadowed” by the events in Ceuta last week.

“These dramatic images beat actually [even] the best statistics and all the numbers I have just given to you on the falling arrivals, the restoration of control … in the eyes of the public across Europe, an event that that last[ed] three days, at one beach in one town, has shaken the politics of an entire continent.”

He says the EU “cannot accept such a breach of integrity of our external borders and violation of the union.”

But he commends Spain and Morocco for their work on “swift returns,” and pointedly praises Spain’s “commitment to prevent any movements towards the mainland.”

“Thanks to these efforts, I think, altogether the integrity of the Schengen are was preserved,” he says, pointing to the fact that Ceuta is covered by a special migration regime with border controls before leaving the enclave towards mainland Europe.

Ukraine moves to develop own ballistic, anti-ballistic system amid delivery shortages

Meanwhile in Ukraine, president Volodymyr Zelenskyy hosted a meeting on the military’s plans to develop their own anti-ballistic capabilities in the face of delivery shortages for existing missile systems.

He said:

”Developing one’s own ballistic capabilities and creating a new anti-ballistic system is a task that only a tiny minority of countries worldwide have managed to accomplish. Ukraine has this capability. Ukrainians have the necessary scientific foundation and expertise. We can state that our weapons manufacturers have also reached the required level. We expect Ukraine to achieve the necessary results in 2026–2027.”

The meeting looked into some of the “key challenges” including “access to critical components” and “technological cooperation with partners.”

“We support and expect more of our partner countries that are capable of contributing to join the Anti-Ballistic Coalition. We also discussed work with partners on PAC-2 and PAC-3 missiles. I expect results from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Ministry of Defense in their communication with the partners that have already been identified and mentioned on numerous occasions.”

Hungary turns off decorative, non-essential lighting amid drought-related energy crunch

In other news, Hungary has switched off decorative and non-essential lighting at all state institutions, including ‌its parliament, most of the Buda Castle and Chain Bridge, at night to save energy as the country battles another heatwave putting strain on its energy system, AP reported.

Record-low water levels on the Danube have forced the country to largely shut down its sole nuclear plant, which uses the river for cooling, ​stretching power supply capacity to its limits.
