The crisis has created tensions among EU nations who share open borders and has sparked criticism of the Spanish government.

The crossings came after Spain's Supreme Court ruled that migrants stopped at sea while trying to reach Ceuta or Melilla cannot be summarily returned to Morocco.

The two exclaves have long been a bone of diplomatic contention between Spain and Morocco, which does not recognise Spanish sovereignty over them.

The number of people swimming to Ceuta at once has raised questions about why Morocco's border guards did not attempt to stop them. Vivas characterised the incident as an "unequivocal attack".

"Our people feel sad, helpless, anxious, fearful and worried - and they have good reason to, because it is inevitable to wonder whether this will happen again," he commented.

Spain's interior minister has said he was working "firmly" with Morocco to avoid further "violations", and that any migrants still in Ceuta would be returned there as soon as possible.

Brunner said the EU should use all the leverage at its disposal, including visa policy and trade, to secure co-operation from Morocco on migration.

Morocco, for its part, has laid blame for the crisis on Spain's Supreme Court, with an unnamed government source briefing local outlets that "the deterrent element of the system collapsed and the dam gave way, not under the weight of the criminals, but of a judge's decision".

Meanwhile, Moroccan media is reporting fresh online calls to co-ordinate another mass border-crossing attempt, similar to what was seen before the influx last week.

News site Hespress reports that posts and messages were circulating on social media to join closed WhatsApp groups, along with hashtags linked to migration to Spain and work opportunities there - despite warnings from Moroccan authorities that people promoting irregular migration could face prosecution.
