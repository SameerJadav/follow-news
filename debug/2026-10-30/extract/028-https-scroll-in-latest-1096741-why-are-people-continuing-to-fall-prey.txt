Chief Justice Surya Kant on Thursday asked why Indians were continuing to fall prey to digital arrest scams despite public awareness campaigns, media coverage and judicial intervention, Live Law reported.

A Supreme Court bench of Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V Mohana was hearing a suo motu case concerning the growing prevalence of such cyber frauds.

During the hearing, advocate NS Nappinai, appointed as amicus curiae, informed the bench of a recent case in which a woman lost Rs 2.5 crore to a digital arrest scam.

An amicus curiae is an impartial legal expert appointed by the court to assist it in complex legal matters, public interest litigation, or cases where a party is unrepresented.

Expressing concern about the prevalence of digital scams, the chief justice asked: “What we are shocked about – why are people not realising.”

“What is wrong with victims?” added Kant. “Are they not normal persons?”

He further remarked: “What kind of person [are you] in a democratic country if you think you are getting arrested on mobile? Hit the mobile on the wall and the arrest goes.”

The amicus curiae submitted that the fear of arrest was a major factor driving persons to comply with the demands of fraudsters. She added that some psychologists had described the pressure exerted on victims as a form of “hypnosis”.

The bench criticised banks and other agencies for failing to put adequate safeguards in place to prevent suspicious transactions linked to cyber fraud.

Kant questioned why banks could not intervene when transactions appeared inconsistent with an account holder’s usual spending patterns, particularly when they had access to the customer’s transaction history.

He asked whether existing regulations permitted banks to temporarily withhold unusual transactions, inform the local police and verify the legitimacy of the transactions before processing them.

Kant emphasised that preventive action was necessary instead of relying primarily on reports after the money had already been lost.

“Report will only be post-mortem,” Live Law quoted him as saying. “What about prevention? [The] moment a transaction comes, why can't it be withheld?”

Attorney General R Venkataramani, representing the government, told the court that steps were being taken to strengthen the system for identifying suspicious transactions. He said banks had improved their ability to flag such transactions in the last six months and that discussions had been held with the Reserve Bank of India to expedite the necessary rule-making.

The Supreme Court indicated that it was considering directions to hold banks accountable for losses caused by cyber fraud where they had failed to exercise due diligence.

Kant said the court was proposing that banks be required to compensate such persons and face prosecution where fraud occurred because of their failure to take appropriate precautions.

The attorney general opposed the proposed approach, submitting that the question of banks’ liability should be addressed by the central bank through an appropriate regulatory framework.

In cases of “digital arrest”, criminals usually orchestrate the fraud by posing as law enforcement officers, often wearing uniforms and making video calls to victims from locations made to resemble government offices or police stations. They demand money for a “compromise” and “closure of a case” against the victims.

In some cases, the victims are “digitally arrested”, with the scammers claiming that the persons are required to be visible until their demands are met.

In January 2025, Scroll published a series of extensive reports about Chinese crime syndicates that run cyber crime centres from Southeast Asia, mainly Cambodia, Myanmar and Laos. These highly sophisticated “scam compounds” are staffed with thousands of people, many of them from India, who are lured with fake job offers and then forced to work on scamming people back home.
