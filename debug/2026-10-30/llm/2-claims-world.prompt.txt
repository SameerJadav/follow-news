How did the Spain train crash happen and what will investigators be asking?

Spain began three days of mourning on Thursday for at least 69 people who died in a high-speed rail collision, as hospitals continued to treat dozens of injured passengers and train drivers went on strike to urge more investment in railway safety.

The trains – one from Spain’s state-owned Renfe and the other from the private rail company Iryo – were travelling from Madrid towards the southern region of Andalucía on Wednesday when they collided just before 9am. After the Iryo train came to a halt, the other slammed into its rear, knocking several carriages off the track near Urda in the central region of Castilla-La Mancha.

Spain’s foreign ministry said 42 foreign citizens from seven countries were involved in the crash, adding that one had been killed and at least seven injured.

The crash came 10 months after 46 people were killed and dozens more injured when two trains – one from Renfe and one from Iryo – collided on the same line near the town of Adamuz in Andalucía.

The disaster has raised fresh questions about safety on Spain’s large, modern high-speed rail network. The transport minister, Óscar Puente, said rear-end collisions such as Wednesday’s were “highly unlikely”, and suggested the accident could have been caused by human error under “inexplicable” circumstances.

Emergency services combed the wreckage through the night for more victims, using cranes to hoist pieces of mangled metal. At daybreak, police once again launched drones to survey the crash site.

Emiliano García-Page, the leader of the Castilla-La Mancha regional government, said on Thursday morning that rescue teams did not think they would find any more bodies in one damaged carriage that was ‌still overturned, but added that body parts had been found that would need to be identified.

“Yesterday we experienced a catastrophe that without a doubt has left all of Spain in shock,” said García-Page. He confirmed that 50 of the people who had been taken to hospitals in the region were still being treated.

The prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, speaking during a visit to the emergency command centre in the city of Toledo, said the authorities were doing all they could to establish the cause of the crash and prevent another rail tragedy.

“I’d like to let all Spaniards know that every institution that is part of the state – from the smallest municipality to the government of Spain – is working day and night, not only to provide a response to the families of the victims, but also, of course, to uncover every last detail of this tragedy and to strengthen safety measures so that something like this never happens again.”

King Felipe, who visited a hospital in Toledo where some of the injured were being treated, said Spain had “once again suffered an extremely harsh blow” and called for lessons to be learned.

“There is obviously an ongoing investigation, which should establish how and why this tragedy happened,” he said on Thursday. “Measures will need to be looked at and adopted so that this doesn’t happen again.”

Meanwhile, Spanish train drivers went ahead with a previously planned strike to press for more investment in railway safety. The crash was Europe’s deadliest train accident since a derailment in Santiago de Compostela in north-west Spain that killed 80 people in 2013.

URDA, Spain — Spain's second high-speed rail disaster this year, a crash in which one train struck another from behind, killing 68 people, has raised fresh concerns about the safety of the country's rail network.

Spain's network of high-speed rail has been considered one of the nation's crowning achievements. It ranks second to China in track mileage in the world. Until this year, it had not endured a deadly crash since coming into service over three decades ago.

The collision Wednesday that turned a sleepy stretch of rail lined by olive groves into a scene of carnage came just four weeks before the country votes in a national election.

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez visited the crash site in the central province of Toledo after regional authorities confirmed that more than 60 people had died and dozens more were being treated at hospitals.

Only in January, 46 people had died when one train jumped the track and slammed into the side of another going the opposite way in southern Spain.

The graphic above shows the placement of derailed train cars from both trains after the crash and the events leading to the crash. Phil Holm/AP hide caption

Sánchez pledged an investigation into the latest collision and improvements to prevent another tragedy like the two that have shaken trust in a rail system that carried 45 million travelers last year, including millions of tourists.

"It is clear that we need to reinforce the safety of our train service to rebuild the trust in our high-speed rail," Sánchez said. "It is not acceptable for a country like Spain to endure two high-speed train accidents in the same year."

Sánchez's government declared three days of mourning starting Thursday.

Both deadly crashes this year involved a train operated by the state-owned RENFE company and one from the privately owned IRYO. Spain only liberalized its high-speed rail service in 2021, with the Italian IRYO starting service the following year.

Sánchez has weathered many crises since taking power in 2018, staying in power and winning reelection after the COVID-19 pandemic and the global rise in inflation.

But polls show he faces another uphill battle when voters cast ballots in Spain's first national election since 2023. The new rail disaster can't help the prospects of his Socialist party.

Sánchez and other progressive parties had hoped to leverage a wave of discontent over housing costs and high rents when he called for early elections earlier this month.

About an hour before news of Wednesday's crash spread, Sánchez had posted on X a message that "There are lots of reasons to be optimistic about this country."

Now it appears he will have to handle concerns over the state of Spain's rail service along with attacks from right-wing parties over his liberal migration policies and accusations of corruption against his party and his wife, which both he and his wife deny.

His transport minister, Óscar Puente, had to handle media questions over whether he would resign following a second major train wreck under his watch. His response was that he was fully focused on dealing with the collision, not his future.

Sánchez's main rival, Popular Party leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo, demanded a full investigation of the crash. The conservative leader said that in respect for the victims his party canceled its campaign events for the day.

"This is the moment to respect the dead, the injured and their families," Núñez Feijóo said. "That does not mean we will ignore the fact that Spaniards are asking tough questions that demand answers."

The far-right Vox leader, Santiago Abascal, who is likely hoping to enter a coalition government with the conservatives, did not show the same restraint.

"More dead. Another tragedy. More rage over being left to fend for ourselves, the despair and the betrayal that Spaniards are enduring," Abascal said just hours after the crash.

"I promise to work tirelessly until this government, which is a criminal organization, is sitting in a courtroom to answer for its crimes," he said.

European Central Bank (ECB) President Christine Lagarde will step down months before her term ends, kicking off a race to succeed her against a turbulent backdrop in the eurozone.

Lagarde announced on Thursday that she will leave the post on February 7, about nine months before the scheduled end of her eight-year term. She said in a filmed statement posted on X that she was leaving “with a sense of good work done and an ECB that is fit for the future”.

The move gives European Union leaders time to choose her successor before April’s French presidential election, which polls have suggested far-right candidate Marine Le Pen is favoured to win.

The timing could allow current European Council members, including French President Emmanuel Macron, to take part in appointing Lagarde’s successor before France’s vote.

Macron, who is not allowed to run again, thanked Lagarde in a post on X for “the determined action she led at the head of the European Central Bank” during “a period marked by numerous crises”.

“The transition at the head of the ECB must now be prepared in an orderly and balanced manner with our European partners,” the leader of the eurozone’s second-largest economy added.

However, Markus Ferber, a German member of the European Parliament, warned: “If the impression arises that such a departure is timed around national elections, it damages trust in an institution that must stand above any domestic political calculation.”

French Finance Minister Roland Lescure praised her tenure. “Christine Lagarde has been an exceptional President of the ECB. As Europe navigated through crises and difficult times, she brilliantly modernised and strengthened the institution,” he posted on social media.

[a3] NPR World <https://www.npr.org/2026/10/29/g-s1-147496/ukraine-ai-drones-war-russia> (FULL TEXT)
A drone operator with the military call sign Professor operates a Lupynis-10-TFL-1 from The Fourth Law at a test site in central Ukraine. Paula Bronstein/for NPR hide caption

KYIV, Ukraine — Like many tech entrepreneurs in Ukraine, Yaroslav Azhnyuk turned to developing drone warfare after Russia's 2022 invasion.

Drones have transformed the way wars are fought. Azhnyuk says artificial intelligence is making drones faster and more capable.

"When the other side has orders of magnitudes higher capabilities, it gives you an asymmetric advantage," he says. "It can make you 10,000 times more effective."

Azhnyuk runs the Ukraine-based defense firms The Fourth Law and Odd Systems. They are among the top companies producing AI-powered drones in Ukraine. Dozens of Ukrainian military units already use their autonomous drone, called the Lupynis-10-TFL-1.

Russia is also deploying AI-enabled drones against Ukraine. AI warfare is part of a high-tech arms race that has many worried, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He has warned that AI could lead decisions on the battlefield as soon as next year.

Azhnyuk told NPR that he acknowledges the concern over destructive weapons but says it's not specific to AI.

"It has been with humanity since the invention of fire or steel," he says. "Any kind of tool can be used for good or for bad. And in the end, it's the human that decides how to use that tool."

Azhnyuk is 37, a gregarious computer scientist who hails from a family of scholars and scientists. NPR meets him in a conference room in Kyiv; for security reasons he asked us not to reveal much else about the location.

He worked for years in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including Petcube, which makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and even entertain pets while they're alone.

"These cameras allow pet parents to watch pets when they're not at home," he says, "and fling treats to them and shine a laser toy for them. We sold a million of these devices that are quite popular in the U.S."

After Russia launched its full-scale war, Azhnyuk, by then living in Ukraine, pivoted to the defense space.

"I was like, OK, I've got to move on from Petcube and do something to protect my country," he says. "And so now we're making not cameras that fling treats but cameras that fling explosives at people who came to occupy our land."

The cameras he's talking about are part of an image recognition system incorporated into first-person-view or FPV drones.

"We are leaders in autonomous drones and thermal cameras," he says.

A Lupynis-10-TFL-1 drone is seen in the air at a test site in central Ukraine. Paula Bronstein for NPR hide caption

The autonomous Lupynis TFL-1 drone combines a small, affordable FPV drone with an autonomous terminal guidance unit. That means the drone has an onboard computer and AI technology that takes control during the last 500 meters (or 0.3 miles) of the flight.

"But it's very different from, say, the kind of AI that sits on those data centers that power Claude," he says. "Or even the kind of AI that does self-driving for your Tesla."

Azhnyuk says this AI technology uses simple tools to recognize images and make smart software "which then allows you either to navigate autonomously without a human or to choose a target autonomously or to fly toward the target autonomously."

The Fourth Law's autonomous drone is named after the 20th century Ukrainian poet and political activist Anatoliy Lupynis, who endured decades of Soviet imprisonment and psychiatric torture. The company named all its weapons after Ukrainian intellectuals and artists killed or persecuted by Russians in the last century.

"It is important for us to help to resurrect those people," Azhnyuk says. "Their ideas are still with us. And now they're fighting back."

The Fourth Law teams test the Lupynis TFL-1 drones every day. We meet one team in an overgrown field outside Kyiv. They've set up a fold-out table near a copse of trees and a hornets' nest.

A slight, sandy-haired man stands up from the fold-out table and says he's the head of the test and integration team. He uses the military call sign Stab. The Fourth Law asked NPR not to reveal this location or the identities of those who work here for security reasons.

"So you see here is our camera, plus the microcomputer inside this 3-D model," Stab says. "All of the mind is here," he adds with a laugh.

The microcomputer inside the drone runs on a deep-learning neural network that uses hours of combat footage to recognize targets.

"Like vehicles, light-armored vehicles, tanks, and flying objects," he says.

The drone begins its flight guided by a pilot, who also asked us to use his call sign, Professor. He navigates the drone with a control stick. He selects a target and checks a video monitor. Then he sees a red flash.

The drone has locked onto its target: a dilapidated car an employee is driving along a dirt road. Professor l
