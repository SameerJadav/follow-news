The Supreme Court on Thursday pulled up the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) over cyberfrauds, saying banks must compensate victims for losses when their employees are found to be involved.

A bench headed by Chief Justice of India Surya Kant noted that digital arrest scams persisted despite public awareness campaigns, media coverage and earlier judicial directions. The court was hearing a suo motu case on the issue.

The court questioned why the banks should not be held accountable for the losses suffered by their customers, when the financial institution can waive loans worth crores as a one-time settlement. “Banks can waive thousands of crores in loans by entering into so-called one-time settlements for ₹5 crore or ₹10 crore. Why should they not be accountable to these victims?”

Attorney general R. Venkataramani opposed implementing such a rule, stating that the liability of banks needs to be addressed by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).

The court’s observations came after senior advocate N.S. Nappinai, assisting the bench as amicus curiae, flagged the continued prevalence of digital arrest scams. She referred to a recent incident in which a woman allegedly lost ₹2.5 crore. In such scams, fraudsters impersonate police officers, government officials or judicial authorities and threaten victims with arrest to force them to transfer money.

The bench also told the banks that the customers have given them access to their account details and transaction history, and they must act as a “protective umbrella” for them. They also raised the question of why banks do not take the necessary steps as soon as suspicions arise or a money-related message appears.

“What we are shocked about is why people are not realizing? What is wrong with the victims? Are they not normal people? This is everywhere in the media,” the bench observed.

The bench also raised issues of bank employees being involved in such frauds. They questioned why RBI was consulting banks on safeguards when some bank employees were themselves allegedly involved in the misuse of accounts.

Venkataramani also told the court that cyberfrauds had been reduced by 50% compared to last year. “The number of digital arrest cases has reduced by about 50% compared with last year because of the court’s intervention, action by ministries and departments, and awareness programmes.”

In August, the Supreme Court directed the RBI to set up a framework and a standard operating procedure (SOP) for banks dealing with mule accounts and cyber-enabled financial fraud. Mule accounts are bank accounts used to receive or transfer money obtained through illegal activities, including cybercrime.

During Thursday’s hearing, the attorney general asked for some more time to get the proposed SOP ready. The court accepted the request and granted time till 30 November to file the proposed Reserve Bank of India Know Your Customer Amendment Directions, 2026. While the court instructed RBI to make it operational by the end of the year.

More than 21,900 complaints of digital arrest fraud involving over ₹647 crore were registered in the first eight months of 2026, according to a 23 October report by Hindustan Times. The figures were part of a status report filed by the Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C) and the ministry of home affairs (MHA) before the Supreme Court, which took suo motu cognisance of the issue a year ago.

The Supreme Court on Thursday said banks and other financial institutions must compensate victims if the fraud is found to have resulted from a failure of due diligence, and called for preventive safeguards to stop suspicious transactions before money is transferred.

A bench comprising Chief Justice of India Surya Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V Mohana said it would examine the Reserve Bank of India's proposed Know Your Customer (KYC) Amendment Directions, 2026, which are expected to be notified by November 30 and operationalised by December 31, news agency PTI reported.

The court stressed that the framework must provide for suitable compensation to victims where financial institutions fail to meet their due diligence obligations.

During the hearing, the CJI also expressed frustration over people continuing to fall prey to digital arrest scams despite public awareness campaigns and media coverage.

"What kind of person you are in a democratic country if you think you are getting arrested on mobile? Hit the mobile on the wall, and the arrest goes," he remarked, as reported by LiveLaw.

The amicus curiae, senior advocate NS Nappinai, told the bench that such scams continued despite earlier interventions and cited a recent case in which a woman lost ₹2.5 crore, the report further added.

She submitted that fear of arrest often drives victims to comply with the demands of fraudsters, with some psychologists describing the phenomenon as a form of hypnosis.

The Attorney General, R Venkataramani, appearing for the Centre and other authorities, told the court that reported digital arrest cases had declined by around 50% compared with the previous year.

The CJI, however, pointed out that losses from such offences had still reached nearly ₹800 crore this year, according to news agency PTI .

The bench stressed the need for preventive measures, including temporarily suspending unusual transactions that deviate from an account holder's established transaction pattern, automated reporting of suspicious activity and physical verification to establish the genuineness of transactions.

"Report will only be post-mortem. What about prevention? Moment a transaction comes, why can't it be withheld?" the CJI asked.

The court also emphasised the need for banks to alert neighbouring police stations about unusual transactions involving customers who may be victims of digital arrest scams.

The bench asked the RBI to submit a list of all authorised financial institutions by the next date of hearing, indicating that it could issue directions applicable to all such institutions without requiring individual banks to be impleaded in the proceedings.

It also sought details of the institutions that would be covered by the proposed guidelines and the measures being considered to compensate victims for their losses.

The court said it would examine the RBI's proposed KYC Amendment Directions, 2026, before issuing further directions.

Chief Justice Surya Kant on Thursday asked why Indians were continuing to fall prey to digital arrest scams despite public awareness campaigns, media coverage and judicial intervention, Live Law reported.

A Supreme Court bench of Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V Mohana was hearing a suo motu case concerning the growing prevalence of such cyber frauds.

During the hearing, advocate NS Nappinai, appointed as amicus curiae, informed the bench of a recent case in which a woman lost Rs 2.5 crore to a digital arrest scam.

An amicus curiae is an impartial legal expert appointed by the court to assist it in complex legal matters, public interest litigation, or cases where a party is unrepresented.

Expressing concern about the prevalence of digital scams, the chief justice asked: “What we are shocked about – why are people not realising.”

“What is wrong with victims?” added Kant. “Are they not normal persons?”

He further remarked: “What kind of person [are you] in a democratic country if you think you are getting arrested on mobile? Hit the mobile on the wall and the arrest goes.”

The amicus curiae submitted that the fear of arrest was a major factor driving persons to comply with the demands of fraudsters. She added that some psychologists had described the pressure exerted on victims as a form of “hypnosis”.

The bench criticised banks and other agencies for failing to put adequate safeguards in place to prevent suspicious transactions linked to cyber fraud.

Kant questioned why banks could not intervene when transactions appeared inconsistent with an account holder’s usual spending patterns, particularly when they had access to the customer’s transaction history.

He asked whether existing regulations permitted banks to temporarily withhold unusual transactions, inform the local police and verify the legitimacy of the transactions before processing them.

Kant emphasised that preventive action was necessary instead of relying primarily on reports after the money had already been lost.

“Report will only be post-mortem,” Live Law quoted him as saying. “What about prevention? [The] moment a transaction comes, why can't it be withheld?”

Attorney General R Venkataramani, representing the government, told the court that steps were being taken to strengthen the system for identifying suspicious transactions. He said banks had improved their ability to flag such transactions in the last six months and that discussions had been held with the Reserve Bank of India to expedite the necessary rule-making.

The Supreme Court indicated that it was considering directions to hold banks accountable for losses caused by cyber fraud where they had failed to exercise due diligence.

Kant said the court was proposing that banks be required to compensate such persons and face prosecution where fraud occurred because of their failure to take appropriate precautions.

The attorney general opposed the proposed approach, submitting that the question of banks’ liability should be addressed by the central bank through an appropriate regulatory framework.

In cases of “digital arrest”, criminals usually orchestrate the fraud by posing as law enforcement officers, often wearing uniforms and making video calls to victims from locations made to resemble government offices or police stations. They demand money for a “compromise” and “closure of a case” against the victims.

In some cases, the victims are “digitally arrested”, with the scammers claiming that the persons are required to be visible until their demands are met.

In January 2025, Scroll published a series of extensive reports about Chinese crime syndicates that run cyber crime centres from Southeast Asia, mainly Cambodia, Myanmar and Laos. These highly sophisticated “scam compounds” are staffed with thousands of people, many of them from India, who are lured with fake job offers and then forced to work on scamming people back home.

The Supreme Court on Thursday criticized the ministry of corporate affairs (MCA) over its handling of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), observing that the ministry had “completely hijacked” its administration, undermining the tribunal’s autonomy and affecting its functioning.

“We are compelled to observe that this has been completely hijacked by the ministry of corporate affairs. That is the problem. And that is impinging on the autonomy of the institution,” Chief Justice of India Surya Kant said.

“The ministry wants to have a complete grip over the institution. And that’s what’s most unfortunate,” the bench added.

The observations came during a hearing of suo motu proceedings examining administrative shortcomings at the NCLT, which adjudicates corporate insolvency proceedings under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC) and disputes under the Companies Act.

The bench also expressed concern that insolvency proceedings were being prioritised over matters arising under the Companies Act, resulting in delays in hearing the latter category. Lawyers representing the NCLT Bar Association told the court that the listing of cases reflected this imbalance.

Mint reached out to the corporate affairs ministry for comment. Its response was still awaited at the time of publication.

In its intervention before the court, the NCLT Bar Association flagged severe shortages of judicial and technical members, saying these were affecting the tribunal’s ability to conduct regular hearings. It said 18 NCLT benches were holding half-day sittings because of a shortage of members. As of 30 June 2026, the tribunal had 26 judicial members and 25 technical members, including its president, against a sanctioned strength of 63, the association said.

The association also raised concerns over stalled renovation work at the CGO Complex, inadequate courtroom facilities, and the absence of permanent support staff. It said 246 proposed support staff positions had not been operationalised, with court masters, bench officers and other personnel continuing to work largely on temporary or contractual arrangements. Frequent outages of the tribunal’s document management system had also disrupted hearings, it alleged.

Senior advocate Sanjiv Sen, representing the NCLT Bar Association, highlighted the deteriorating infrastructure at the tribunal’s principal bench in Delhi on Thursday. He told the court that floors had been shut because of poor building conditions, leaving courtrooms struggling to function effectively.

Attorney general R. Venkataramani told the court that appointment recommendations were in the pipeline and proposed a joint meeting involving government officials, the NCLT president, and bar representatives to address the concerns.

The Supreme Court subsequently asked the presidents of the NCLT and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) to submit comprehensive proposals identifying their infrastructure, staffing and administrative requirements.

The court’s intervention comes against the backdrop of prolonged corporate insolvency proceedings. An Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) report marking a decade of the IBC said ongoing corporate insolvency resolution processes (CIRPs) averaged 713 days as of March 2025, with around 78% exceeding the statutory 270-day timeline. The report identified judicial delays, litigation and multiple proceedings among the key challenges to timely resolution.

The Economic Survey 2025-26 also highlighted the scale of the problem, noting that nearly 30,600 cases were pending before the NCLT as of March 2025. Average CIRP duration stood at 713 days, more than twice the 330-day outer limit envisaged under the IBC, while cases completed in FY25 took an average of 853 days. The survey also noted that only 30 NCLT benches handle both IBC and Companies Act matters, adding to capacity constraints.

Structural ties endure, but US President Donald Trump’s flip-flopping on energy policy has done lasting damage to Washington’s relationship with New Delhi, says Chietigj Bajpaee from the Chatham House think tank.

LONDON: United States Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s visit to India last week triggered two opposing narratives: one that the India-US relationship remains in chronic decline, and another that it continues to move from strength to strength. As with most things, the reality is somewhere in the middle.

The naysayers point to Rubio visiting Mumbai and Jamnagar but not New Delhi.

This overlooks the fact that many other foreign leaders have bypassed the Indian capital: Chinese President Xi Jinping visited the southern state of Tamil Nadu in 2019, as part of their informal summit diplomacy; United Kingdom Prime Minister Keir Starmer visited Mumbai last October after India and the UK signed a free trade agreement; and German Chancellor Friedrich Merz visited India’s IT hubs Ahmedabad and Bengaluru in January.

As argued in earlier analysis, the India-US relationship remains “sticky”. Despite the war of words between New Delhi and Washington over the last year – fuelled by tariffs, sanctions, and tightening visa rules – structural engagement remains strong.

Bilateral trade in goods and services grew to around US$240 billion in 2025. India continues to conduct more joint military exercises with the US than any other country. Technology cooperation remains robust, with the launch of a joint satellite in 2025, and US tech companies pledging massive AI investments in India.

Rubio’s latest visit sought to double down on this economic engagement. The trip to Jamnagar – home to the world’s largest refinery, owned by Reliance Industries – came amid continued oil price pressures triggered by the US-Israel war with Iran.

Rubio’s visit was rooted in India becoming the second-largest buyer of Venezuelan heavy crude – most of which is processed in Jamnagar – after the US captured Nicolas Maduro in January. It also followed Trump’s March announcement that Reliance will invest in a major refinery in Brownsville, Texas.

And yet, those who buy into the Trump administration’s rhetoric – from Rubio claiming that India is a “refining superpower” to Trump calling Narendra Modi “one of my greatest friends” and US Ambassador to India Sergio Gor referring to Kashmir as an “important part” of India during his visit to the disputed territory in August – miss the point that there has been lasting damage to the India-US relationship.

Talk is cheap, particularly under the Trump administration’s fickle foreign policy.

Nowhere is this more apparent than in energy policy, where the US flip-flopping on India’s purchase of Russian crude has been nothing short of staggering.

India initially came under heavy scrutiny following the Russian invasion of Ukraine, before Western countries came around to understanding India’s strategic compulsions as a price-sensitive developing country that was playing a stabilising role in global energy markets.

New Delhi assumed things would only get better under Trump, given his proclivity to engage Moscow, but instead his administration slapped 50 per cent tariffs on India and threatened secondary sanctions. White House trade adviser Peter Navarro infamously labelled India a “laundromat for the Kremlin”.

In February, the US reduced tariffs on India as the framework for a US-India interim trade deal was reached in exchange for New Delhi suspending Russian oil purchases. But this was partially reversed when the US allowed India to buy Russian oil stranded at sea following disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz triggered by the US-Israel attack on Iran in the same month.

Then, in September, the Lindsey O Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, authorised tariffs up to 100 per cent on the five largest buyers of Russian oil and gas, including India. But with the US now buying Russian diesel itself, Trump waived those penalties in October, with Rubio indicating a 60-day review to follow.

The fundamental problem with the Trump administration’s approach to India is that it prioritises short-term interests (in this case quelling inflationary pressures ahead of the upcoming US midterm elections) over long-term strategic considerations.

Two key pillars of the India-US relationship have weakened under Trump: the countries’ shared democratic values and India’s role as a counterbalance to China. The values debate is non-existent in Trump’s might-is-right, hemispheric worldview, and the “China factor” has lost salience, as each country is independently seeking to reset relations with Beijing.

Earlier this year, Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau said the US would not repeat the “mistake” it had made with China by allowing India to emerge as an economic competitor to the US. This was a far cry from the stance of previous US administrations, which helped to facilitate India’s rise.

India's trade agreements went beyond tariff reductions, rules of origin and market access, encompassing services, investments, education, skill development, technology partnerships and cooperation on climate goals, Goyal said at the fourth National Conference on India's Industrial Transformation, organised by the Institute for Studies in Industrial Development (ISID) and the Industrial Development Research Association (IDRA).

He said nine free trade and bilateral trade agreements concluded by the government had opened preferential market opportunities in economies with a combined gross domestic product (GDP) of $60 trillion, compared with $10 trillion covered by agreements concluded before 2014.

Goyal also defended the government's decision to withdraw from the China-backed Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), saying that joining the free trade bloc would have sounded the death knell for India's manufacturing ambitions.

"Otherwise, we would have been a nation of salesmen and salesgirls marketing Chinese goods in India," Goyal said, referring to India's withdrawal from RCEP negotiations on 4 November 2019, in Bangkok.

The minister argued that the agreement, which brought together China, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand and the 10 ASEAN countries, would have effectively opened India's domestic market to Chinese products without adequate tariff protection.

He said the earlier agreements were largely with countries competing with India, while the current approach sought to foster complementary economic relationships. India’s trade agreements aimed to create opportunities for both sides, with India exporting products its partners needed and importing goods required for its own development, he said, describing such arrangements as win-win partnerships.

Goyal said trade was fundamentally about comparative advantage and competitiveness rather than absolute numbers alone. He observed that several developed countries had structured their tariff schedules to impose high duties on labour-intensive goods, using them as negotiating instruments.

India’s trade negotiations also sought to protect sensitive sectors and facilitate access to high-technology products, including graphics processing units (GPUs), memory technology and medical technology required for the country’s development. The agreements were being negotiated after extensive stakeholder consultations to safeguard India’s sensitivities and interests, he said.

Goyal identified quality, global market access, economies of scale and inclusivity as the four key factors that would drive India’s industrial transformation. Reiterating Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s emphasis on “Zero Defect, Zero Effect”, he said Indian manufacturers must focus on quality and use opportunities created by trade agreements to expand their global presence.

India’s domestic market of 1.4 billion people, combined with access to international markets, could help manufacturers achieve economies of scale, affordable production and competitive costs, he said.

The minister highlighted growing international recognition of Indian talent and skills. Recalling a conversation with Germany’s foreign minister regarding the immigration agreement between the two countries, Goyal said he had been told that Germany needed substantially more Indian talent than the 90,000 Indians required annually under the arrangement.

He cited Indian professionals in senior positions at Fortune 500 companies and their contributions to cutting-edge technologies as evidence of the capabilities of the country’s workforce. Goyal expressed confidence that India would remain the world’s fastest-growing major economy for another 20-25 years, attributing this prospect to the talent, skills and energy of its people.

On climate action, Goyal said global efforts should be guided by the principles of polluter pays and common but differentiated responsibilities, with adequate financial and technological support for developing countries. He said 60% of India’s installed electricity capacity came from green and renewable sources, while coal remained essential for energy security and affordable power.

The minister said the Make in India initiative and phased manufacturing programmes across sectors had helped bring factories to India and increase domestic production. India had moved from being predominantly a buyer of products manufactured elsewhere to becoming a country increasingly recognised for manufacturing, he said.
