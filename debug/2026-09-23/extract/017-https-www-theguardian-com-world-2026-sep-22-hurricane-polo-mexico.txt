Polo, with sustained winds of 165mph, is churning 215 miles south of Zihuatanejo but is unlikely to directly hit Mexico

A tropical storm off the south-west coast of Mexico has intensified into a category 5 monster in fewer than 24 hours, fueled by stronger than usual El Niño weather patterns in the Pacific Ocean.

Hurricane Polo was sending out maximum sustained winds of 165mph (266km/h) on Tuesday as its center churned about 215 miles (346km) south of the coastal city of Zihuatanejo, in Guerrero state.

On Monday, Polo was only a tropical storm with winds below 74mph (120km/h). Its rapid and sudden growth is testament to the dominance of this year’s so-called super El Niño, which set a record on Monday as the most extreme on record, and has amplified a number of extreme weather events globally.

Forecasters at the National Hurricane Center in Miami said Mexico was likely to avoid a direct hit, but warned that further intensification was likely, and that life-threatening flooding and mudslides were possible inland, along with dangerous surf and rip current conditions.

El Niño occurs when extended ocean temperatures in the eastern and central Pacific, and near the equator, rise above their historical average.

Meteorologists say the phenomenon increases disruptive winds and curtails other conditions favorable to storm development in the Atlantic basin, which remains quiet with less than three months to go in this year’s hurricane season.

Conversely, it promotes increased activity in the Pacific, where the 2026 El Niño remains one of the strongest on record.

“The water temperatures in two separate areas, the north-central Pacific and off the Mexican coast, are running well above average,” Paul Pastelok, AccuWeather’s lead long-range meteorologist, said in the online weather service’s fall hurricane outlook last month.

“We are seeing a strong impact in both the Atlantic and Pacific basins, with extreme wind shear limiting Atlantic storm development, while the Pacific remains quite active, as we expected back in the spring.”

Hurricane Polo’s single-day intensification also fits into the assessment of University of Miami senior research associate Brian McNoldy on his tropical Atlantic blog that El Niño, which he said was only “moderate” towards the beginning of September, was “forecast to become very strong, likely the strongest on record, by later this fall and into winter”.

Polo is the 18th named storm, and seventh hurricane, of the 2026 Pacific season, which runs from 15 May until 30 November.

By contrast, the Atlantic basin, which has a six-month season from June until November, has seen only six named tropical storms and no hurricanes so far.

It is the first time in 112 years that no Atlantic hurricanes have formed by 21 September, and 2026 could join 1907 and 1914 as the only years without one being recorded.
