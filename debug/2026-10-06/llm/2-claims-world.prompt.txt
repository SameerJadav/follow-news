Progressive voters and supporters of incumbent president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva left reeling after far-right surge

Flávio Bolsonaro is in pole position to win Brazil’s presidential election, after a far-right tsunami propelled him to a shock first-round victory over the incumbent, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, leaving progressive voters reeling.

The 45-year-old rightwing candidate received 56.1m votes to Lula’s 53.8m – 47.03% to 45.16% – meaning the 80-year-old leftwinger has a mountain to climb if he is to bounce back in the second round on 25 October. Never, since the return of democracy to Brazil in 1985, has the first-round runner-up managed to turn the game around and win the second ballot.

“Miracles are possible in politics. But I don’t see that happening,” the political scientist Miguel Lago said of Lula’s chances of snapping back. “I don’t see Flávio losing this election now.”

Speaking outside the White House on Monday, Donald Trump told reporters: “Well, it’s a big victory: close but a very, very big victory. Amazing, actually.”

Trump, who is a longtime ally of the Bolsonaro family, predicted the runoff would be “a very close election”.

The far-right surge was a triumph – albeit an incomplete one – for the Bolsonaro clan and its 71-year-old patriarch, Jair Bolsonaro.

The disgraced ex-president, who is serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup after losing the 2022 election to Lula, saw four relatives elected to congress. His 44-year-old wife, Michelle Bolsonaro, won a seat in the senate, as did his second son, Carlos Bolsonaro, who is 43. Bolsonaro’s youngest son, 28-year-old Jair Renan Bolsonaro, and the ex-president’s 62-year-old brother, Renato Bolsonaro, both won seats in the lower house of congress.

Across Latin America’s biggest democracy, key congressional and gubernatorial races were also won by staunch Bolsonaristas including Tarcísio de Freitas, who was re-elected as the governor of São Paulo state, and Sérgio Moro, who was elected governor of the southern state of Paraná. Both men were ministers during Bolsonaro’s 2018-22 government.

Bolsonaro’s Liberal party (PL) will now boast the largest caucus in the senate with 28 of its 81 seats. Combined with the seats of other hard-right parties, that number rises to 43, giving the movement an upper house majority.

Meanwhile, pro-Lula senate candidates, including the former environment minister Marina Silva, the leftwing veteran Benedita da Silva, and the centrist former planning minister Simone Tebet, suffered bitter defeats.

Nikolas Ferreira and Lucas Pavanato, two young Bolsonarista social media warriors, became the best-voted congresspeople in Brazilian history, securing more than 3m votes.

“We are experiencing a revolution … The far right is growing in a very consistent way, election after election,” said Lago, calling the election the worst for progressive and moderate centrist parties since the late 80s.

Lago believed Lula had managed to interrupt the advance of Bolsonaro’s populist movement in 2022, thanks to positive voter memories of his first two terms, from 2002 to 2010. Back then, a global commodities boom allowed Brazil’s first working-class president to fund major social welfare programmes that helped millions escape poverty. “But it was just an interregnum,” Lago said.

Control of the senate should allow pro-Bolsonaro politicians to approve an amnesty for their movement’s charismatic leader, allowing the former president to emerge from the Brasília mansion where he is living under house arrest and reassert his leadership of the far right.

“I think he will be freed and I think he will be very important during Flávio Bolsonaro’s [presidency],” said Lago. “Flávio will be running the country and [Jair] Bolsonaro will be doing what he has always done: leading the revolution.”

The senate majority may also help Bolsonaro’s third son, Eduardo Bolsonaro, return from self-imposed exile in the US. The 42-year-old, who has close ties to Donald Trump’s Maga movement, was sentenced in absentia to four years in prison earlier this year for coercion – the result of his lobbying the Trump administration to interfere in his father’s coup trial. Eduardo Bolsonaro reportedly hopes to become foreign minister if his brother wins the presidency.

The result is good news for Trump, who has been accused of trying to sway the election in Bolsonaro’s favour, and his attempt to expand the conservative “orange wave” sweeping Latin America to its biggest democracy and economy. Recent years have seen a succession of pro-Trump presidents win power in the region, from Costa Rica, Honduras and El Salvador to Chile and Colombia.

One of Trump’s most enthusiastic regional cheerleaders, the Argentinian president, Javier Milei, celebrated Bolsonaro’s “historic” victory against “the communist Lula”. “The American continent belongs to citizens who want to live in freedom,” Milei wrote on X.

Karl Deisseroth from The Howard Hughes Medical Institute and Stanford University in the United States developed a way to turn the protein into a light-controlled switch for nerve cells.

## Alex Jones pushes ‘anti-white civil war’ claim

In the US, far-right conspiracy theorist Alex Jones has shared lengthy video compilations about the protests, using them to promote an Islamophobic narrative.

Jones has claimed that France is experiencing an “anti-white civil war”, with “dead bodies everywhere”. There is no evidence to support those claims. There has been no deaths reported to dates in the current protests, and the demonstrations are centred on concerns over the state of France’s [education](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/education/) system, including large class sizes, teacher shortages and poor school conditions, not [immigration](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/immigration/).

Jones uses a series of videos to illustrate his claims about supposed immigrant violence. But some of the footage predates the current protests by years. For example, one of his video compilations begins with footage of a burning bus, but it's video of a school bus that caught [fire](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/fire/) in the southern French city of Nîmes in 2022, with no casualties reported. It's thus being used out of context to push Jones' narrative.

Jones has also claimed that Catholic institutions and clergy are being targeted during the protests. In one video, he says he has seen"at least a dozen videos of Catholic priests being set on fire"

We found no evidence supporting these claims in connection with the current student protests.

The videos used by Jones to support his wider narrative of an “anti-white” uprising are also not evidence of an organised campaign against Catholic institutions or clergy.

## Tommy Robinson shares old protest footage

Similar claims have been circulating in the [UK](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/united-kingdom/). Far-right activist Tommy Robinson has shared footage of unrest in France while promoting an anti-immigration message.

In one post, Robinson described a video as evidence of a “green/Islamic holy alliance” spreading through Western Europe and the United States, claiming that what is happening in France would spread to other countries.

But the footage is not new. The video shows aftermath of a [protest](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/protest/) in Paris and has been circulating online since 2022. It therefore cannot be used as evidence of the current student demonstrations.

Robinson has also posted about what he calls "occupied France," sharing footage showing violence and looting during recent demonstrations.Some of this footage is genuine and does show incidents linked to the current protests. But the fact that the footage is authentic does not substantiate the broader claims about immigration being made, and these videos are being misused to create a racist and xenophobic narrative.

## ‘France has fallen’ video is from a football celebration

The footage, which has been viewed millions of times, shows smoke around the [Eiffel Tower](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/eiffel-tower/) and large crowds gathering in the streets, but was filmed in May, when Paris Saint-Germain fans gathered to celebrate the club’s [Champions League](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/champions-league/) victory.

Several hundred schools in France closed totally or partially on Monday amid security fears following a wave of nationwide student protests, the education minister has said.

The protests by pupils complaining of poor conditions began at one Paris school on September 17 but last week mutated into sometimes violent demonstrations across the nation.

A 15-year-old boy in the northern town of Lens sustained a severe injury to his hand amid one of the protests on Monday. French news agency AFP cited a police union source as saying he appeared to have picked up a sting ball grenade, another term for a type of tear gas grenade also often called a blast ball, and it exploded in his hand.

The demonstrations have started to dominate France's political debate just a few months before presidential elections that will choose a successor to Emmanuel Macron, who is approaching term limit.

Education Minister Edouard Geffray said on Sunday that lessons would be totally or partly suspended in some 400 to 500 high schools on Monday, as the security conditions "have not been fulfilled."

According to Geffray, the schools were being closed mainly as a precaution, with more large protests planned for Tuesday.

Geffray said the number of schools remaining closed on Monday would be similar to that on Friday but that there was planned to be a "partial, progressive return to lessons."

He said 24 schools had been "burned or ransacked" and 78 education staff members injured since the protests began.

"There are activists — particularly from the far left — who have infiltrated the high school student movement, hijacked it from the students themselves, and engaged in extreme violence," Geffray said.

"Clearly, this poses a risk to both our staff and our students," he added.

Valerie Pecresse, the president of the Ile-de-France region surrounding the capital, said that more than a third of the 468 schools in the region would be shut on Monday.

The protesting teenagers at French upper high schools, called Lycees, have complained of high rates of absenteeism by teachers, school days that are too long and dilapidated facilities.

They have also protested at the lack of adequate cooling on hot summer days, with some towns seeing temperatures of 40 degrees Celsius (104 Fahrenheit) last summer in a country without widespread air conditioning.

Organizers have called for the protests to continue on Tuesday, saying the government was still failing to propose "concrete measures" to remedy the situation.

On Sunday, Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu urged local authorities to make it a priority to fill gaps left by long-term absences and unfilled posts.

He also called a meeting for Tuesday with regional presidents to discuss renovating school buildings that are often in poor condition.

The protests have been marked by several violent incidents, with security forces sometimes using tear gas and batons to disperse the young protesters.

More than 5,000 people, 85% of them minors, have been arrested since the start of the movement.

On Sunday, a 16-year-old was detained after a 59-year-old teacher was hit and doused with gasoline on the sidelines of a protest in the southern town of La Ciotat.

The French branch of rights group Amnesty International last week described the action taken by police as "disproportionate."

Interior Minister Laurent Nunez has, however, defended security forces, saying they were "not cracking down on young people" but rather "protecting" school and university students wishing to "peacefully voice their demands."
