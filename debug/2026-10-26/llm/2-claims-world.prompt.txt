=== STORY 0 [world] — Lula da Silva wins historic fourth term as president of Brazil, defeating Flávio Bolsonaro ===

[a0] France 24 <https://www.france24.com/en/americas/20261026-brazil-s-president-lula-da-silva-beats-flavio-bolsonaro-to-win-re-election> (FULL TEXT)
Brazilian President [Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/luiz-inacio-lula-da-silva/) won an unprecedented fourth term on Sunday, the country's electoral court said, in a shock result that defied polls, precedent and global anti-incumbent sentiment to put the brakes on a wave of right-wing election victories across [Latin America](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/latin-america/).

Lula, an icon of the global left and the most dominant force in [Brazilian politics](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/brazilian-politics/) this century, defeated Senator [Flavio Bolsonaro](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/flavio-bolsonaro/), the eldest son of former President [Jair Bolsonaro](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/jair-bolsonaro/). The senator had finished first in the election's first round and hoped to revive his father's far-right movement.

At times, Lula's reelection looked like a long shot, as voters soured on him and his Workers' Party after years of [corruption](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/corruption/) scandals and rising prices, while sweeping societal changes left the former metalworker and union leader looking out of touch with an increasingly app-based [economy](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/economy/) and its growing ranks of informal workers.

Lula's victory marks another comeback in a career full of them. The 80-year-old was jailed from 2018 to 2019 on corruption charges that were later overturned, paving the way for him to beat Jair Bolsonaro in the 2022 election.

"He is a statesman, a giant," leftist federal lawmaker Erika Hilton, who was with Lula in Sao Paulo during the vote count, told Reuters.

## Unlikely path to victory

Pollsters said better-than-expected turnout had powered Lula to victory. Leonardo Barreto, the co-founder of consultancy Think Policy, said voters had chosen Lula's familiar embrace over Bolsonaro's novelty.

"People today are choosing between change, with some apprehension, and a known path, which guarantees certain rights," he said.

During the campaign, Lula pointed to a track record of falling [poverty](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/poverty/) and inequality, while [unemployment](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/unemployment/), illiteracy and [deforestation](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/deforestation/) hit record lows during his third term.

He warned Bolsonaro would cut [welfare](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/welfare/) spending, backslide on social advances and hand over [Brazil](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/brazil/)'s sovereignty to US President [Donald Trump](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/donald-trump/), whose State Department led a campaign to get the Bolsonaros back to power.

Lula's win deprives Trump of a major addition to what has been a growing bloc of friendly right-wing Latin American governments that have embraced Washington's agenda on security and migration.

Celso Amorim, a former Lula foreign minister, told Reuters the leftist "would be very important for establishing a point of balance in Latin America. Lula is that balance."

## Comeback king

Lula becomes the first candidate since Brazil's return to democracy in the 1980s to be elected after finishing second in the first round.

Lula, who was president between 2003 and 2011, campaigned on a potent mix of pragmatism and [populism](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/populism/) that has shaped his career in national politics for half a century, rolling out a slew of reforms aimed at improving the lives of workers and poor Brazilians.

These gained steam with late-campaign pledges that ranged from free nationwide weekend public ​transport, which has to be formally submitted to Congress, to a ‌15% increase in his trademark welfare program that is already being implemented.

Lula also championed a constitutional amendment that became Brazil's biggest labor reform in decades, which passed with wide support in Congress.

## Challenging legislative reality

Still, Lula's fourth term could be his most difficult yet, after Brazil elected its most conservative Congress since returning to [democracy](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/democracy/) in the 1980s.

Combined, Bolsonaro's Liberal Party (PL) and allied parties could control over 280 seats in the lower house and up to 50 seats in the Senate, enough for comfortable majorities in both chambers.

"He is likely to be a lame duck from the start," said Oliver Stuenkel, a professor of international relations at the Getulio Vargas Foundation in São Paulo.

The incoming legislature will likely deepen recent trends of presidents ceding power to Congress, Stuenkel said, predicting Lula will face waning influence over Supreme Court nominations, unwieldy party coalitions, and even the threat of [impeachment](https://www.france24.com/en/tag/impeachment/).

Full report: Lula wins fourth term as Brazil’s president over Flávio Bolsonaro

Brazilian president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has staged a dramatic election fightback and defeated his far-right opponent Flávio Bolsonaro to claim a historic fourth term in power.

With nearly 99% of votes counted the 80-year-old leftist received 51.75% of the vote to his 45-year-old rival’s 48.25%, Tom Phillips and Tiago Rogero report.

Lula narrowly lost the first round of the election to Bolsonaro in early October, casting his political future into doubt and plunging progressive Brazilians into three weeks of despair. Never, since the return of democracy in the 1980s, has the first round runner-up won the second vote.

But Lula did precisely that on Sunday, prompting joyous scenes on the streets of São Paulo, where he had been following the count, and at the hotel where he was watching the count.

For Lula, who turns 81 on Tuesday, the victory represents a sensational final chapter in a storied political career that began when he emerged as a union leader in São Paulo’s industrial suburbs in the late 1970s.

Brazil’s electoral court has just announced that leftist president Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva defeated far-right senator Flávio Bolsonaro and was elected president of Brazil for a fourth non-consecutive term.

Lula is the first candidate in Brazil’s democratic history to overturn a first-round defeat in a runoff.

[a3] Channel News Asia <https://www.channelnewsasia.com/world/brazil-pauses-presidential-vote-count-after-supreme-court-extends-voting-hours-6490261> (FULL TEXT)
The election pits leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva against right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of convicted former President Jair Bolsonaro.

BRASILIA: Brazil's Supreme Court extended voting in the country's presidential election by two hours on Sunday (Oct 25), leading the electoral authority to pause a vote count that had already started publishing early-stage results.

The election pits leftist President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva against right-wing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of convicted former President Jair Bolsonaro.

Polls were initially set to close at 5pm Brasilia time (4am Monday, Singapore time) and will now close at 7pm after Supreme Justice Cristiano Zanin accepted a request by Lula's campaign to extend voting, pointing to public transportation issues in three cities in the state of Bahia.

Zanin's decision landed right around the 5pm cutoff, when the electoral court had already started publishing results that showed Lula leading Bolsonaro with 0.25 per cent of ballot counted. Lula had 57.66 per cent of votes, compared with 42.34 per cent for Bolsonaro, the early count showed.

After the Zanin ruling, the TSE said it would pause the vote count to allow for the extended voting window to close.

The pause over the vote count injects uncertainty into a Brazilian electoral process still grappling with the fallout from 2022, when Jair Bolsonaro refused to concede defeat to Lula. His supporters later invaded government buildings in protests against the result, and Bolsonaro received a 27-year prison sentence for plotting a coup.

Lula had asked electoral authorities to extend voting in at least nine states after his campaign alleged some officials reduced election day public transportation, impacting voters' ability to get to the polls.

Earlier on Sunday, a court in Bahia, a Lula stronghold in the Northeast, ordered the arrest of a top transportation official in the city of Salvador over allegations that he directed a reduction in the city’s bus fleet.

Bolsonaro had also asked for voting to be extended in at least three states, citing bad weather.

[a4] Livemint <https://www.livemint.com/news/world/us-midterms-2026-political-ad-spending-tops-10-billion-where-is-the-money-coming-from-and-what-is-driving-the-surge-11792930396568.html> (FULL TEXT)
Spending on political advertising for the 2026 US midterm elections has crossed $10 billion, setting a new record for a midterm election cycle.

The total is expected to reach $11.6 billion by Election Day, according to media tracking firm AdImpact, as reported by TIME magazine.

The projected spending is higher than the $8.9 billion spent during the 2022 midterms but short of the $11.2 billion recorded during the 2024 presidential election cycle.

The surge comes as Republicans and Democrats compete for control of Congress, with wealthy donors and political groups spending heavily to influence key races, TIME reported on October 19.

Republicans have spent more than Democrats on television advertising in several closely contested Senate races. Across nine of the tightest Senate contests, Republicans spent $233 million on broadcast television ads last month, compared with $121 million for Democrats, according to AdImpact data cited by TIME.

Across Senate races classified as toss-ups by the Cook Political Report, Republicans have spent or reserved $947 million for advertising, compared with $625 million for Democrats. These figures include ads booked for future broadcasts as well as those already aired.

The gap is smaller in House races. Republicans have outspent Democrats by $14 million across 21 toss-up contests.

Texas has become one of the most expensive Senate races this election cycle. Combined advertising spending and reservations for future airtime have crossed $500 million, according to AdImpact.

Democrat James Talarico is challenging Republican Ken Paxton in a contest that could affect the balance of power in the Senate. Talarico raised about $50 million more than Paxton between July and September. However, Republican-aligned outside groups have spent heavily on advertising supporting Paxton.

The world's richest person, Elon Musk, described by TIME as the biggest individual donor to federal elections in the current cycle, contributed $158 million to the Senate Leadership Fund last month. The group transferred a matching amount to Texas PAC, which supports Paxton. Musk also donated $10 million to Lone Star Liberty PAC in August.

The money did not go directly to Paxton's campaign. Instead, it was channelled through outside political groups that can fund advertising independently of candidates.

Ohio's Senate race has drawn more than $450 million in combined advertising spending, according to AdImpact. Republicans have spent nearly $270 million supporting Jon Husted, while Democrats have spent more than $180 million backing Sherrod Brown.

Husted was appointed to replace JD Vance after Vance became vice-president in 2025. The winner of the special election will need to contest the seat again in 2028 to secure a full six-year term.

Maine has also seen heavy spending, with around $200 million committed to advertising. Republican Senator Susan Collins is seeking a sixth term against Democratic challenger Troy Jackson. Republican groups have spent or reserved nearly $114 million for advertising since January, compared with about $81 million for Democrats.

Political action committees (PACs) collect contributions to support or oppose candidates. For the 2025–26 election cycle, individuals can contribute up to $3,500 per election to a federal candidate's campaign and up to $5,000 annually to a traditional federal PAC. Separate limits apply to donations to national party committees.

Super PACs operate under different rules. They cannot donate directly to candidates or coordinate their advertising with campaigns, but they can raise unlimited amounts from eligible donors to fund independent political advertising.

This allows wealthy donors to spend large sums supporting or opposing candidates through outside groups, even when direct contributions to candidates are capped.

The projected $11.6 billion covers political advertising, not the full cost of running campaigns. Staffing, travel and voter outreach add to election expenses.

Research published in the American Political Science Review in 2021 estimated that a Senate candidate with a $2 million advantage in television advertising could gain about 10,000 votes. In a close contest, that number could matter.
