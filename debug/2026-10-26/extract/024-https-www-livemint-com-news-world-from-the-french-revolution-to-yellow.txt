France’s long tradition of street protests is once again in focus as hundreds of thousands of people have taken to the streets over deteriorating school conditions and the rising cost of living, reviving questions about the role of mass mobilisation in the country’s political life.

France’s Ministry of the Interior said 410,000 people protested nationwide on Saturday, including 110,000 in Paris.

In central Paris, businesses remained shuttered and shop windows were covered with protective panels, while some retailers hired private security. At least 24 metro stations were closed at police request.

From the French Revolution and the Paris Commune to May 1968 and the Yellow Vests, protests have repeatedly turned public grievances into national political issues. They have also sometimes led to confrontation with the state.

Major cultural institutions, including the Louvre and the Musée d’Orsay, also shut for the day, while Art Basel Paris cancelled its weekend programme.

Authorities deployed more than 70,000 police officers and gendarmes across France, seeking to prevent a repeat of the violence that marred demonstrations on 17 October. What began largely peacefully escalated into looting, vandalism, and clashes, leaving thousands of businesses damaged or ransacked. More than 2,000 people were taken into police custody, according to official figures cited by agencies.

The roots of this tradition stretch back to the French Revolution of 1789, when popular unrest helped bring down the old order. The storming of the Bastille on 14 July became a defining symbol of resistance to royal authority and the power of ordinary people to challenge an entrenched political system.

The 19th century saw repeated uprisings in Paris, including the June Rebellion of 1832, later immortalised in Victor Hugo’s Les Misérables. The Paris Commune of 1871, a radical revolutionary government that emerged after the Franco-Prussian War, was violently suppressed by government forces. Its defeat left a lasting imprint on the country’s labour movement and revolutionary tradition.

These episodes reinforced the central place of protest in French political history, even as they exposed the risks of confrontation between demonstrators and the state.

One of the most consequential modern examples came in May 1968, when student demonstrations against university conditions and wider social constraints escalated into a nationwide labour movement.

Millions of workers joined strikes, bringing much of the country to a standstill and posing a major challenge to President Charles de Gaulle’s government. The crisis eventually led to the Grenelle agreements, which included significant wage increases and improved labour conditions.

May 1968 became a reference point for subsequent generations of protesters, reinforcing the belief that sustained collective action could reshape public policy.

The Yellow Vests movement of 2018-19 offered a more recent illustration of the power of street mobilisation. Initially triggered by fuel tax increases, the protests broadened into a revolt against the cost of living, economic inequality and the perception that political decision-making favoured urban elites over people in smaller towns and rural areas.

The movement put pressure on President Emmanuel Macron’s government and prompted concessions, including the cancellation of planned fuel tax increases and additional measures aimed at easing household pressures.

Like earlier protests, the Yellow Vests movement showed how a specific policy grievance could become a wider expression of distrust in political institutions.

As France confronts rising living costs and concerns about public services, the demonstrations draw on a long historical legacy.

From the Bastille to May 1968 and the Yellow Vests, protest has repeatedly served as a mechanism through which grievances become national political questions in France.
